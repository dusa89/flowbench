The constraints: this agent is triage — never a dispatcher, never 911 — and calls get announced

Two hard gates, and both come before any automation is built. Gate 1 — triage, not coverage: the AI classifies the call and routes it; it does not guarantee emergency service. A burst pipe at 2am gets escalated to your on-call rotation under your rules, and your emergency-fee schedule belongs in the estimate paperwork, never promised on a phone call. Do not present this system as 24/7 emergency coverage — a classification error on a gas-leak call is a liability event, not a software bug. Verify your state's contractor-board rules and your liability coverage with counsel before going live. Gate 2 — recording consent: the agent announces the call is recorded (or your state says otherwise — many states are two-party consent, so the announcement-first pattern is the default). Build both gates into step 0; the playbook below assumes they're in place. This page is not compliance advice.

The short version

Our contractor missed-call playbook (#1) catches the calls you miss — this playbook makes sure nothing ever misses. The phone rings at 11pm, the AI answers on the first ring, decides in about a minute whether this is an emergency or a tomorrow problem, wakes your on-call tech for the real ones, and books a morning slot for the rest. The demand case is the strongest we've scored in this family: 35% of plumbing calls are emergencies, 91% of emergency jobs go to the first contractor who answers, the industry misses 58% of incoming calls, and 40% of emergency calls land after business hours — about 61 after-hours calls a month per plumbing business, worth $17,500+/month in unanswered revenue (Waverly Research, September 2026, reported via ABNewswire 2026-09-10; PHCC National Association president quoted on record). The build is Synthflow (the live-answer AI voice agent — classification, escalation, morning-slot booking) with ElevenLabs voices so a 2am caller hears your office, not a robot, and Make as the dispatch engine behind it (on-call rotation routing, escalation chain, CRM/dispatch-board sync, emergency-fee schedule logic). Step 0 — Write your emergency rules before you automate anything

0The triage definition your whole shop agrees on

An AI can't triage what your shop never defined. Before any tool is bought, the owner writes the emergency definition list — the actual calls that wake a human: gas smell, no heat below freezing, active water leak, sewer backup, no AC with an elderly resident, sparking panel. Everything else is next-day. This list is the playbook's brain; every downstream step references it. Then the escalation SLA: who gets woken first, how long they have to answer (the honest bar is 3–5 minutes), who is second, and what happens when nobody answers (the honest answer is a bonded overflow option or the morning queue — write it down instead of discovering it at 2am). Shops that skip this step get an agent that classifies "correctly" against nobody's rules — step 0 takes one whiteboard session and prevents every argument the system will ever start.

Built with: a shared doc (the emergency definition list + rotation roster + escalation SLA), stored where the whole shop can read it. No software yet — the list IS the product until step 1.

Step 1 — The agent answers on the first ring

1Live pickup, classification in one minute

After hours, every inbound call routes to the voice agent instead of voicemail. It does four things in roughly a minute: announces itself as your shop's after-hours line (recording announcement first, per the gate above), asks what's happening, classifies against your step-0 list, and branches — emergency goes to step 2, everything else goes to step 3. The classification script is deliberately narrow: it asks symptom questions ("is water actively flowing right now?"), never diagnosis questions, and anything ambiguous escalates up, not down — a false emergency costs a callback; a missed emergency costs a customer and worse. Unanswered escalations (the on-call tech doesn't pick up) route per your step-0 SLA, and the caller gets a concrete commitment ("our tech will call you back within 10 minutes") instead of a voicemail box. One Central Florida shop running this pattern reports a 2.8x increase in after-hours lead capture with 80+ active customers (Anvoa, launched mid-2025), and 62% of their callers never realize they're speaking with AI.

Built with: Synthflow (the voice agent — classification script, branching, escalation triggers) + ElevenLabs (the voice: calm, unhurried, your shop's tone at 2am — the voice is half the customer experience on an emergency call). Bench route: n8n self-hosted if the shop wants the stack on its own infrastructure.

Step 2 — The emergency path: dispatch, not just notification

2Wake the rotation, log everything

This is the step that separates a triage playbook from an answering service: the emergency path dispatches. When the agent classifies a real emergency, Make fires the rotation — call the on-call tech, text if no answer in the SLA window, escalate to the second tech, log every attempt with timestamps to the dispatch board. The caller gets a case reference and a callback commitment; the tech gets the classified symptom summary, the address, and the callback number — not a raw voicemail to decode at 2:07am. Emergency-fee logic lives here too: the rotation knows which calls carry the after-hours rate, and the fee is disclosed when the tech calls back, never promised by the agent. Honest note on the load: this is the heaviest step in the playbook. Your rotation probably lives in a group text, your dispatch board in ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, or a whiteboard. Mapping rotation → automation is a day of real work before the first 2am call arrives — budget it, don't hand-wave it.

Built with: Make (the dispatch engine: rotation routing, SLA timers, escalation chain, dispatch-board sync, timestamped logging) reading the rotation from wherever it actually lives (ServiceTitan dispatch, Housecall Pro, JobNimbus, or — to start — a shared sheet the office updates). Ugly and shipped beats elegant and theoretical.

Step 3 — The routine path: morning slots, not missed calls

3Book the morning, sync the board

Non-emergency callers never hear "call back during business hours." The agent offers the next real morning slots from your schedule, books one, texts a confirmation with the tech's arrival window, and syncs the booking to your CRM/dispatch board so the morning crew sees a full board, not a voicemail pile. Every booking, classification, and recording lands in a morning handoff queue the office reviews at open: emergencies dispatched overnight (with the full timestamped log), morning bookings, and the ambiguous calls that need a human listen. The handoff queue is also the quality loop — misclassifications get corrected against the step-0 list, and the list gets sharper every month. Shops running the callback pattern from our playbook #1 keep both: #1 catches daytime misses, this playbook owns the night. That pairing — callback for the day, live answer for the night — is the whole phone strategy on one page.

Built with: Synthflow (booking flow, confirmation texts) + Make (CRM/dispatch-board sync, handoff-queue assembly). Calendar source is your real schedule (Google Calendar, ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro) — the agent only offers slots that actually exist.

Common objections, answered honestly

We already have an answering service — why rebuild this?

A human answering service takes a message; this system dispatches. The difference shows up at 2am: the service emails you "caller reports water in basement," and your tech wakes up to decode it. The dispatch path classifies against your rules, wakes the right tech in the rotation, logs every attempt with timestamps, and gives the caller a case reference and a callback commitment. If your current service already does all of that, keep it — this playbook is for the shops still getting raw voicemails at open.

Won't customers hang up when they realize it's AI?

At 2am with water coming through the ceiling, customers care about one thing: someone picked up and help is moving. One operator running this pattern reports 62% of callers never realize they're speaking with AI (Anvoa, 2026). The realistic failure mode isn't "they detected AI" — it's a misclassification, which is why ambiguous calls escalate UP and the morning handoff queue exists. Measure misclassifications monthly; that's the quality metric, not caller sentiment.

Our on-call rotation is a group text — does this still work?

Yes — that's the honest starting point for most shops. The Make back end can read a shared sheet the office updates when the rotation changes; ugly, but it ships. The integration mapping to ServiceTitan or Housecall Pro dispatch is the upgrade path, not the entry requirement. What doesn't work is no rotation at all — if "whoever hears their phone" is the current system, step 0 (writing the actual rotation down) is the deliverable, and the automation comes second.

Isn't this the same as your missed-call playbook (#1)?

They're the two halves of one phone strategy. #1 is reactive: a call was missed, the system calls back within 60 seconds. This playbook is preventive: after hours, no call ever misses — the agent answers live, classifies, and dispatches. Different trigger (live ring vs missed-call event), different skill (emergency classification and dispatch escalation vs speed-to-callback), different automation map. Run both and the phone line has no dead air, day or night.

What about liability on emergency calls?

The real question, and the reason the hard-constraint box sits at the top of this page. The agent is triage — it classifies and routes under your written rules; it never guarantees emergency coverage, never promises response times on the call, and never quotes emergency fees (those go in the paperwork when the tech calls back). Classification errors are the risk: ambiguous calls escalate up by design. Verify your state's contractor-board rules, your liability coverage, and your call-recording obligations with counsel before going live. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice.
