The price-increase truths: five gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before you send the letter. Gate 1: the number is yours — compute it from your own burden rate. "72% of contractors are raising rates" is demand evidence, not your price. Copying a percentage from the internet is how shops under-raise and have to send a second letter six months later. Gate 2: one increase per year, announced with weeks of notice. Two increases in a year reads as sneaky — build the cushion into this one. Gate 3: honor every quote already accepted. The new rate goes into the next estimate, never the current job. Gate 4: check plan-member and contract terms before you announce. Rate-locked maintenance members get the grandfather window, not a surprise. Gate 5: consent before any AI voice call. The announcement campaign runs on the same permission discipline as every other voice playbook in this library — TCPA/CASL rules apply. None of this is financial advice; the math below is yours to run with your accountant.

The verdict: audit the drift, cushion once, announce like a professional

The system: audit your real loaded labor cost and find the drift between the burden rate in your estimating template and the burden rate in your actual 2026 books, compute the one annual increase that closes the drift with cushion, write the announcement with a thank-you, a brief honest reason, and an open door, deliver it in tiers — plan members first with 30 days' notice, the full list with a price-lock window that creates honest urgency — update the pricebook in one bulk pass, and hold the line on objections with the compare-the-scope script. The directional evidence (third-party, dated, below) says nearly every HVAC contractor raised prices in the last year — the shops that lose customers aren't the ones who raise, they're the ones who raise badly. The verdict that counts is a letter your best customer reads and shrugs at.

1Run the burden-rate audit — find the drift in your own books

The increase starts with your real loaded labor cost, not a survey number. Pull one tech's fully loaded cost for 2026: hourly wage, workers' comp, health insurance, PTO, vehicle and fuel, tool replacement, uniform and training. Divide by billable hours, not clock hours. Now open your estimating template and find the burden rate it's using — if you built it two or three years ago, it is lying to you. The gap between the template's number and the real number is your starting increase, before you touch any survey figure. The practitioner's 35%-vs-45% example is not your number; yours comes out of your payroll and your comp renewals. What to file: the one-page burden card (per-trade loaded rate, template rate, drift, date) lives next to the pricebook — every estimate renders against the card, not the template.

Tooling: the burden card is a spreadsheet today. Make can pull the payroll and comp-renewal inputs into one annual review report when the manual pass proves the format.

2Compute the increase — the cushion math that makes one increase per year stick

Gate 2 says once a year — so this increase has to survive twelve months of cost drift. Take the burden drift from Step 1, add the material trend you actually buy (not the national average — your supply house invoices), and add cushion: if the drift says 6%, announce 8% so you are not writing a second letter in June. Translate the percentage into dollars the customer can see — "about $12 on a standard service call" beats "an 8% adjustment" — and translate it into dollars the owner can feel: 8% on the labor line of a $400K-revenue shop is the difference between the margin you planned and the margin you got. The number you announce is the number you computed, rounded up, announced once. Run it past your accountant before it leaves the shop — that is what they are for.

Tooling: the flat-rate pricebook from Playbook #24 is the input here — this playbook updates the numbers inside it; it does not rebuild it.

3Write the letter — thank-you, brief why, open door

The letter is four sentences and a date. Thank them for the business (name the relationship length for your top tier), state the new rate and the effective date, give one brief honest reason — rising labor and material costs — and leave the door open for questions by phone. What the letter never does: apologize for charging a sustainable rate (the trades guides are explicit on this), blame the economy or the government, or bury the effective date. Plan members and contract customers get their version first with 30 days' notice and the grandfather window spelled out; the full customer list gets the same letter a week later. Keep one template with the merge fields (name, tenure, current rate, new rate, date) — personalization is the line between "another price hike" and "they clearly still value working with me."

Tooling: the merge template lives in your CRM or email tool. Make fills the fields from the customer record so the owner is not hand-typing 400 letters.

4Run the announcement machine — tiers, lock window, voice calls to the top tier

Delivery order matters. Day 1: plan members and contract customers (email + the contract-terms check from Gate 4). Day 3: the full active list (email, then text for the non-openers). Day 5: the top 100 customers get a personal call — an AI voice campaign (Synthflow + ElevenLabs) delivers the same thank-you-first script the owner would, at a scale the owner can't: name, tenure, new rate, effective date, one question — "anything I can answer?" The pricebook updates in one bulk pass the same week (Make scenario against the pricebook file from Playbook #24), so quotes rendered after the effective date carry the new numbers automatically. The honest urgency engine: a price-lock window — "any work booked before [date] locks in your current rate" — is in every version of the letter. It is real (booked work is booked work) and it turns the announcement into your best estimate-booking week of the quarter.

Tooling: Make (bulk pricebook update + the email/text drip sequence), Synthflow + ElevenLabs (the top-100 announcement call campaign — consent-gated per Gate 5).

5Hold the line — the objection scripts

Expect a mixed reaction: most customers say nothing, some want to talk it through, a few push back hard. The compare-the-scope script: "I understand you need to compare costs — my quote includes the materials, the work-area protection, the testing, and the work described. Send me the other scope and I'll check whether there are differences." The never-discount rule: if a longstanding customer is genuinely strained, offer the scaled-back version of the work at a lower scope — never the cheaper version of the same work. Reducing the specification is safer than cutting the margin the job needs. The walk-away permission: being too expensive for some customers is fine if the number is right — the increase selects the customers you can serve well. And the last action of the week: put next year's review on the calendar — the same month, every year, announced the same way. The cadence is the brand.

Tooling: the objection scripts live one page behind the letter template in the office binder — the dispatcher and the CSR read the same lines the owner does.

Questions shops actually ask

Won't I lose customers if I raise prices?

Some may leave — and that is priced into the system. The shops that lose customers aren't the ones who raise; they're the ones who raise badly: no notice, an apology-soaked letter, a surprise on the invoice. A fair increase delivered with weeks of notice, a thank-you, and an open door keeps the customers who value the work — and selects out the ones whose jobs never carried margin anyway.

Is there a standard percentage I should raise?

No — there is only your number, computed from your burden rate and your material costs in Step 1 and 2. Industry figures (the 5% wage moves, the 6.7% material moves) are the weather, not your thermometer. The shop that copies "8%" from a blog under-raises or over-raises — both are expensive. Run your own math, round up once, announce once.

When is the best time to announce an increase?

Once a year, on the calendar — early December notice for a late-January effective date is the standing cadence in the small-business guidance, because it gives customers a full planning cycle and lands before the busy season. The worst time is never; the second-worst time is a surprise line on next week's invoice. Pick a month, keep it, and the cadence itself becomes part of the brand.

Should I explain the reasons — tariffs, inflation, insurance — in detail?

One brief honest sentence: rising labor and material costs. Customers don't need your P&L and they don't want a civics lesson; a short reason makes the increase feel fair, a long one makes it feel defensive. Never apologize for charging a sustainable rate — the moment the letter sounds ashamed of the number, the customer starts negotiating it.

What about my maintenance-plan members — their rates are locked?

Gate 4: check the plan terms before you announce. Rate-locked members get the grandfather window spelled out in their version of the letter — "your plan rate holds through [date]" — plus the personal call tier, because members are your best customers and the worst ones to surprise. When the lock expires, they join the next annual cycle like everyone else.
