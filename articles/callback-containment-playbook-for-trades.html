The discipline gates: the catch call hunts defects, it never practices the trade — three gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before the first catch call goes out. Gate 1: the AI catch call is triage, never diagnosis. It opens a callback ticket; it never quotes coverage, never promises a free fix, and never declares whose fault the defect is over the phone. Coverage is decided by the shop's written warranty terms, and a licensed tech confirms every defect on site — the voice on the phone is the intake clerk, not the tech. Gate 2: consent and call-recording rules. Permission for the follow-up call is captured at booking (the family pattern), the AI identifies itself as automated in the first sentence, and two-party-consent states get the recording announcement. The five steps

1The 48-hour catch call: one question, one branch

Forty-eight hours after every finished job, the AI calls the customer: "This is [shop]'s automated check-in — is everything working the way it should?" One question, one branch. Yes hands the customer to playbook #6's review-capture path — the same review link everyone gets, no gating, no pressure. No opens the callback ticket on the call: what is wrong, since when, and when the customer can be home. Script discipline is the whole step: the call ends the moment the customer says all good. Consent for the follow-up is captured at booking, the AI self-identifies in the first sentence, and the opt-out is honored without a second ask.

Tool: Synthflow or ElevenLabs voice on a Make sequence (job-close trigger → 48-hour timer → call → ticket or review handoff). Family legs, verified Oct 1-2, 2026 — no new program needed.

2Ticket every callback against the original job

Every defect gets one callback ticket: job number, tech who did the work, defect description, parts used, labor hours, truck rolls. The cost codes to the original job, never to overhead — the Simpro fix: uncoded return visits are how a 0.38% rework line hides the real number. A callback that isn't coded to the job that caused it makes the scoreboard in Step 5 fiction. The ticket stays open until the customer confirms the fix, not until the tech drives away.

Cost: a spreadsheet row per callback to start. The shared log needs six columns: job, tech, defect, parts, hours, bucket (Step 3).

3Classify and charge-back: three buckets

Every closed callback lands in one of three buckets. Workmanship feeds the per-tech callback scoreboard and the training loop — repeat patterns by tech are coaching material, not blame material, and Step 5 posts them monthly. Parts/manufacturer files the manufacturer warranty claim — this is where the TaskTag $45,000 insight lives: documented defects are recoverable; undocumented ones are donations. Customer-education is the not-a-defect bucket: the "how it works" visit, scheduled on purpose, never rushed — a confused customer who pays for a no-defect visit is a review risk. Bucket 3 visits stay scheduled, not squeezed between real calls.

Compound: #24's pricebook sets the price on any billable callback work; this step decides which bucket the ticket belongs in before the price is ever quoted.

4The callback SOP: same-tech-first, 24-hour SLA

Four rules, non-negotiable. (1) Same-tech-first dispatch — the tech who did the work owns the fix and learns from it; next-available only when the tech is off the schedule or the customer is hot. (2) Parts pre-staged — the callback truck pulls from #23's van logic, never from a supply-house run; the $220 supply-house run on a free job is the leak doubling down. (3) 24-hour response SLA — callbacks outrank new quotes on the board, because the angry clock is the reputation clock and an unanswered callback becomes a one-star review at $285-$850 a pop. (4) One owner per ticket — from open to customer-confirmed close, one name owns the callback; nobody's callback is everybody's callback.

Automation note: the Make sequence that opened the ticket in Step 1 also fires the SLA timer — 24 hours to first touch, escalated to the owner at hour 20.

5The monthly callback scoreboard

Once a month, the owner posts three numbers: callback rate (callbacks per 100 jobs), cost per callback (loaded labor + parts + truck rolls — the honest number, not the tech's hourly wage), and the repeat-offender list (jobs, techs, and equipment models that keep reappearing). Direction matters more than level: the leak shrinks quarter over quarter, and the manufacturer-recovery column in Step 3's bucket gets its own line so the shop sees what documentation recovered. The 90-day test: the numbers are moving down, or the scoreboard is theater. The write-up that matters most is the recovered-margin line — the TaskTag math, in the shop's own hand, posted where the techs can see it.

This is the step that compounds: every classified callback is a training input (bucket 1), a recovery claim (bucket 2), or a process fix (bucket 3) — and every one of them shrinks next month's pile.

Questions the shop will actually ask

Won't the catch call annoy customers?

One question, 30 seconds, ends the moment the customer says all good. Annoyance comes from sales calls wearing check-in costumes — this call is never a sales call, the AI says so in the first sentence, and the opt-out at booking is honored without a second ask. Shops that run it report the opposite of annoyance: the customer who gets a defect caught feels looked after, not sold to.

What if the callback isn't our fault?

Then it's bucket 3 — customer-education. Still a scheduled visit, still logged, still dispatched with the same SLA. The "not our fault" ticket is the one that most needs the ritual: a confused customer who pays for a no-defect visit without a clear process becomes a review risk, and the education visit is the cheapest truck roll you'll ever run.

Should we charge for callbacks?

Your written warranty terms decide that before the playbook starts — the playbook logs the decision, it doesn't make it. Never surprise-charge a callback: the Step 5 scoreboard makes the policy visible so the decision gets made once, on paper, instead of argued per ticket in the driveway.

Same tech or next available?

Same-tech-first: they know the job, they own the fix, and they learn from it — that is how bucket 1 turns into training instead of repetition. Next-available when the tech is off the schedule or the customer is hot. The 24-hour SLA outranks the assignment: the reputation clock doesn't care who drives.

How is this different from the review-capture playbook?

#6 harvests finished jobs for reviews; this hunts defects. #6's object is the review, its trigger is job completion, its metric is review count and rating. This one's object is the callback ticket, its trigger is the 48-hour catch call, its metric is callback rate and cost per callback. Same 48-hour moment, different job — and #6's unhappy path feeds this playbook's intake, by design.
