The discipline gates: a change order is unbilled scope wearing a disguise — three gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before you capture a single change. Gate 1: the signature-before-work rule is the whole playbook. 97% of trade contractors start work on some change orders before they are approved, and that is exactly the leak (Dodge Construction Network + Clearstory, June 2026). The one exception: emergency health-and-safety work gets done first and documented within 24 hours — that is a documented exception, not the default. Gate 2: mechanics lien rights vary by state. A signed change order is your best defense when payment goes sideways, but preliminary-notice rules and lien deadlines are state law — run your form past a construction attorney before it becomes the shop standard. Gate 3: the headline research is commercial construction, vendor-sponsored. The Dodge + Clearstory study is a nationwide survey of GCs and specialty trade contractors, and Clearstory sells change-order software — the commercial focus and the sponsor are disclosed here, and this page re-scopes the discipline for residential service trades, where the change is a $180 extra valve, not a $180,000 structural revision.

The five steps

1The trigger list: define "a change" in writing

Unbilled scope starts as a disagreement about what was included. End the disagreement on paper, once. Write the shop's change-order trigger list and laminate it for every crew: (a) any customer request not in the signed scope; (b) hidden conditions the walkthrough couldn't see; (c) plan-to-field or code conflicts; (d) extra trips the original schedule didn't include; (e) the "while you're here" add-on. The rule the crew memorizes: if it is not in the signed scope, it is a change. Post the list in the van and read it at the quote — customers who hear the rule up front almost never fight the form later.

Cost: one printed page. The trigger list is the cheapest document in this playbook and it does the most work.

2The field capture ritual: 3 minutes, on the spot

The moment a trigger fires, the tech runs the capture ritual before the work continues: (1) two photos of the condition; (2) a one-paragraph scope note on the phone — what changed, why, what it will take; (3) a price estimate on the spot. For sub-two-hour work, a time-and-materials ticket is the capture form. Nothing leaves the jobsite undocumented — the 7-week average approval cycle starts the day the tech fails to write it down, not the day the office submits it. The ritual is under 3 minutes; the write-off it prevents is the month's margin.

Cost: the phones the crew already carries. The shared change-order log can start as a spreadsheet — one row per change, owner, amount, signature status, date sent.

3Price it from the book — never at a discount

Every change order pulls its price from the flat-rate pricebook (playbook #24): loaded labor plus parts plus the trip fee, no haggling math in the driveway. Changes are never discounted — the customer asked for more, not less, and discounting the change teaches the customer that the scope has no price. If the shop doesn't have a pricebook yet, #24's loaded-labor audit is the prerequisite; pricing changes off gut feel repeats the exact error the Dodge study found in 91% of disputed GC payments — disputed pricing from thin documentation.

Compound: this step is where playbook #24 pays off twice — the book that prices the bid also prices the change, which is why the book gets built first.

4The 48-hour written-approval chase

The captured change becomes a one-page change-order form — scope, price, signature line — texted or emailed to the customer the same day. The chase: follow up at 24 hours and 48 hours, then stop asking and start deciding. While unsigned, the base job continues and the changed work stops. The 97%-start-before-approval figure is the habit this step kills. Every form, signed or not, lands in the shared log so the owner sees the unsigned pile every Monday morning. Automation note: Make or your existing CRM can move a captured change into the log and fire the 24/48-hour reminders automatically — the tool is optional, the chase is not.

Free path: spreadsheet log + phone reminders. Automation path: Make sequence from the capture note to the log to the reminders (verified legs from the family bench — no new program needed).

5The monthly bad-debt scoreboard

Top-tier trades track change-order KPIs at more than twice the rate of the rest (55% vs 26%), and that tracking is the habit this step installs. Once a month, the owner posts four numbers: change orders issued, signed, paid, and written off. The write-off rate gets posted next to the dollar total it represents — that number is the leak, in dollars, on one page. The 90-day target: write-offs under 5% of issued change revenue. Whatever is still unsigned at 30 days gets one of three decisions: collect it as a T&M invoice (feeds playbook #9's recovery ladder), negotiate it down with a signed settlement, or write it off with the reason recorded so the trigger list in Step 1 gets smarter next month.

This is the step that compounds: every write-off with a recorded reason is a trigger-list improvement, and every improvement shrinks next month's pile.

Questions the shop will actually ask

What if the customer refuses to sign?

Then you have learned something worth more than the change: the unsigned form goes in the log, the changed work stops, and the base job continues. Refusals are almost always a Step 1 failure — the trigger list was never read at the quote. Log the reason; the list gets smarter.

Is a text-message approval enough?

Better than nothing, and in many disputes it is evidence — but the playbook wants the signed form, because "he texted OK" argues worse than a signature. Keep both: the text is the timestamp, the form is the agreement.

What about emergency work at night?

Gate 1's documented exception: do the work, document within 24 hours, bill it on the emergency rate from the pricebook. Emergencies are why the exception exists — they are not why the rule gets skipped the other 29 days of the month.

Won't this slow the crew down?

The capture ritual is under 3 minutes. The 7-week average approval cycle and the 77% write-off rate are the actual drag on the shop — 3 minutes of documentation is the fastest labor the tech performs all week.

We only do small service jobs — do change orders apply to us?

Yes — the residential version of the change order is the $180 extra valve, the second trip, the "while you're here" add-on. The amounts are smaller and the volume is higher, which is exactly why the capture ritual matters: ten unbilled $180 changes a month is a tech's salary.
