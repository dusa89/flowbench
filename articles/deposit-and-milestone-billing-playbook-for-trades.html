The deposit truths: five gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before you price the next job. Gate 1: the deposit is the only money that is truly yours. Everything after it is a hope until the check clears — the schedule below exists to shrink the hope window. Gate 2: the deposit cap is the law, not a pricing tactic. California limits home-improvement deposits to 10% or $1,000, whichever is less; Pennsylvania caps upfront collection at one-third of the contract price; Maryland caps it at one-third too — and every other state has its own number. Exceeding the cap is a licensing-board complaint, not a strong close. Gate 3: never let the schedule make you the customer's bank. If materials are 40% of the job and your deposit legally caps at 10%, the gap is closed by the schedule — progress payments tied to verifiable triggers — not by floating the customer's project on your credit card. Gate 4: milestone triggers must be owner-verifiable, never contractor-declared. "Fifty percent complete" is an argument; "rough-in inspected, signed, and photographed" is a trigger. Gate 5: this is a money-timing playbook, not a law license. Deposit caps, progress-payment rules, and consumer-protection statutes are state-specific — run the schedule past a construction attorney before it goes into your contract.

The verdict: audit the cap, then build the schedule that closes the gap

The system: audit your state's legal deposit cap and write the progress-payment workaround into the contract (in California that means the first progress payment lands "due upon start of work"), write the 3-milestone schedule — deposit, a verifiable mid-job trigger, and a completion holdback — into every bid, run the milestone-invoice machine (auto-invoice on trigger, owner sign-off photo-logged, T-48 nudge on unpaid milestones), and pull the final payment the week of completion with the walkthrough checklist, the 5-to-10% holdback strategy, and same-day payment rails. The directional evidence (third-party, dated, below) says contractors die from payment timing, not pricing — three-quarters report real cash-flow trouble while fully booked. The verdict that counts is a live job that is never cash-negative.

1Run the deposit audit — know your cap before you write the next contract

The cap table is the foundation everything else stands on. Pull your state's number this week: the maximum deposit a contractor may legally collect on residential/home-improvement work. If you work multiple states, keep a one-page cap card per state — the numbers do not rhyme (10% or $1,000 in California, a third in Pennsylvania and Maryland, and different rules again on commercial work, which most consumer-protection deposit statutes don't cover). The audit also finds the workaround your contract needs: where the cap is low, the first progress payment is written "due upon start of work" — legal progress payment, not a bigger deposit. One California shop's exact cadence, quoted by name: the $1,000 deposit, then progress payments "due upon start of work," with 5-10% held for final. What to file: the cap card (state, cap number, source date, attorney initials) lives in the contract template folder — no contract goes out until the template matches the card.

Tooling: Make or n8n — the contract-generation trigger checks the job's state against the cap card and refuses to render a deposit line above the legal number.

2Write the 3-milestone schedule into every bid — deposit, verifiable trigger, holdback

The schedule is three lines in the contract, and they do all the cash-flow work. Line 1: the deposit (capped at your state's number). Line 2: one mid-job payment tied to an owner-verifiable trigger — "rough-in inspected and signed off," not "50% complete." The trigger needs a photo and a signature, because the invoice that follows it needs both. Line 3: the completion holdback, 5-10% of the contract, due at the final walkthrough. Three working shapes, all operator-tested: the uncapped thirds (33/33/34 on deposit / midpoint / completion), the grow-up ladder (a Texas shop moved from 50% deposits to 25/25/25/25 as ticket sizes grew, because a 50% deposit on a $60,000 job asks the customer for $30,000 of trust), and the capped-cadence (small legal deposit, progress gates at demolition-complete and rough-complete, holdback at finish). The script that sells it: "This is how every job we run is structured — it keeps us from financing your project, and it keeps you from paying for work you haven't seen." The customers who fight the schedule are telling you the payment story upfront — price the risk or pass.

Tooling: Make or n8n — the bid template auto-renders the schedule block with the right cap numbers per state; the milestone triggers become checklist items in the job board, each with its sign-off gate.

3Run the milestone-invoice machine — the trigger fires the invoice, same day

Milestones die in the office, not on the job. The rule: the day the trigger is met and signed, the invoice goes out — the photo-log of the completed milestone is attached to the invoice, which is why the customer signs the trigger card in the first place. Net-7 terms on milestone invoices (the customer agreed to the schedule when they signed the contract; the terms are not a new negotiation). Unpaid at T-48: the automated nudge — email first, then the voice call that references the signed trigger by name. The machine's scoreboard metric is milestone DSO: days from trigger-met to money-in, per job, posted weekly. Shops that invoice on the trigger collect mid-job payments in days; shops that invoice "when the office gets to it" discover the milestone in the aging report. The automation is the enforcement: the job board's milestone card cannot be marked complete until the invoice record exists.

Tooling: Make or n8n — milestone card completed fires the invoice draft with the photo-log attached, starts the T-48 nudge sequence, and flags the card red when terms cross; Synthflow or ElevenLabs for the voice nudge that names the signed trigger.

4Pull the final payment the week of completion — walkthrough, holdback, same-day rails

The final 5-10% is the most expensive money on the job — it's pure margin, and it's the money customers are most tempted to sit on. The pull has three parts: the walkthrough checklist done the day the work finishes (every item signed, defects fixed or priced before you leave), the warranty registration presented as the completion ritual (the customer signs for the warranty and the final invoice in the same sitting), and same-day payment rails — card on file authorized at the walkthrough, not an ACH invoice mailed into the weekend. The weekend gap matters here most: a Friday-signed completion paid by ACH can sit unfunded until Tuesday, and a $12,000 holdback in limbo over a long weekend is a payroll problem. The house rule: no final invoice leaves the shop by mail — it's authorized on the tablet at the walkthrough or it's a callback. The warranty-as-carrot also de-fangs the quality objection: the holdback isn't a threat, it's the warranty's down payment.

Tooling: Make — the walkthrough-completed trigger fires the final invoice, the card-on-file authorization request, and the warranty registration in one sequence; same-day-funding processors enter the build-vs-buy math below.

5Keep the cash-flow scoreboard — never cash-negative on a live job

The ritual that makes the other four stick. Every Friday, one number per live job: money in minus materials-plus-labor out, to date. A job that is cash-negative mid-way is normal only if the schedule says the next milestone fixes it this week — negative with no milestone in sight is the alarm. Two more numbers on the same board: milestone DSO (Step 3's metric, trending up or down) and the schedule-hit rate (what fraction of milestone invoices went out the same day the trigger was met). The house standard, posted on the wall: no live job ends the month cash-negative unless a named milestone lands within 7 days. This is the metric the 74% of struggling shops don't track — they track revenue, and revenue is what the pipeline reports while the cash quietly leaves. The scoreboard is the difference between "we did $200,000 last month" and "we can make payroll."

Tooling: Make or n8n — Friday-morning job-cash snapshot compiled from the accounting feed and the milestone tracker, pushed to the owner as a one-screen report; no dashboards to open.

Build vs buy

Build (the $0 start): the state cap card, the 3-milestone contract schedule template with its owner-verifiable triggers, the trigger sign-off card, the walkthrough checklist, and the Friday cash scoreboard — a folder of documents and one weekly habit. That alone wins the three fights this page exists for: the over-the-cap deposit, the invoice that waits for the office, and the final payment that drifts into next month. Buy the automation (when you run more jobs than you can invoice by hand): Make or n8n for the milestone-trigger invoice fan-out, the T-48 nudge sequences, and the cash snapshot (~their standard tiers); Synthflow or ElevenLabs for the voice nudge on unpaid milestones (~their standard per-minute voice pricing — confirm current rates on their sites). Buy the rails (when weekend volume justifies it): same-day and weekend funding processors — compare the per-transaction cost against what the 60-hour Friday-to-Tuesday gap actually costs you in credit-line interest and payroll float, not against zero. Buy the expertise (once, before the schedule goes into your contract): a construction attorney reviews the deposit-and-schedule language for each state you work in — the caps have edge cases this page doesn't cover. What you never buy: contractor cash-advance products priced like payday loans against your own receivables. The schedule is cheaper.

Honest weak points

First: the cap can sit below your material cost, and the schedule narrows the gap but can't erase it — special-order materials with long lead times may need the customer to buy direct or a materials-only progress payment written around the cap, attorney-reviewed. Second: deposit caps are consumer-protection law aimed at residential work — commercial contracts play by different rules, and this page's cap numbers don't apply there. Third: the schedule is a contract negotiation, and some customers balk — the script in Step 2 is doing real work, and it doesn't work on everyone; a customer who refuses any deposit is data, not a debate. Fourth: same-day funding costs money — the rails earn their fee only when the weekend gap is actually costing you more; a shop with a cash cushion may rationally choose the free 60-hour float. Fifth: this playbook doesn't collect overdue invoices — it prevents them. The money that's already 60 days past due belongs to Playbook #9; the machine that failed to invoice belongs to Step 3.

Questions shops actually ask

How is this different from the lien-rights preservation playbook?

Playbook #30 preserves the legal lever — the preliminary notices, the deadline calendar, the waiver discipline — your right to force payment when everything else fails. This playbook gets the money in on time so the lever stays in the drawer. Same war, opposite ends: #30 is the backstop, this is the front line. And they connect mechanically: #30's Step 4 waiver ledger runs on this page's milestone schedule — the waiver you sign at each milestone matches the payment the milestone released.

How is this different from the overdue-invoice recovery playbook?

Playbook #9 chases money after the terms are crossed — the escalation ladder for invoices that aged anyway. This playbook builds the schedule so there is nothing to chase: the deposit covers materials day one, the milestones invoice on the trigger, the machine nudges at T-48. #9's ladder starts where this page's machine stopped firing. Run both: this page to keep the aging report empty, #9 for the jobs where the machine wasn't running yet.

I'm a subcontractor. My problem is 56-day pay applications from the GC, not deposits from homeowners. Does this apply?

Half of it does. The deposit section is owner-contract work, and subs mostly don't get deposits — your deposit equivalent is the mobilization payment negotiated in the subcontract, which is worth asking for on every job over 30 days. The milestone-invoice machine applies in full: invoice on the pay-app date the day the work is accepted, not when the office batches. And the cash-flow scoreboard is how you learn which GC pays in 56 days and which pays in 25 — then you bid accordingly.

We work in three states with three different caps. Do we need three rituals?

Three cap cards, one ritual. The audit in Step 1 is per-state; the schedule template, the trigger machine, and the scoreboard are identical in shape with different numbers. The contract generator is the enforcement — it renders the right cap per job state, so nobody has to remember whether this job is the $1,000 one or the one-third one.

The customer refuses to pay any deposit. What then?

Walk, or price the risk explicitly. The FTC's own guidance says deposits are normal and full payment up front is the scam pattern — a customer who won't put down even a legal 10% is telling you the payment story before the work starts. If you take the job anyway: shorten the schedule (weekly milestones, not monthly), take the card on file at signing, and don't order special materials until the first milestone clears. Then run the scoreboard twice as often.
