The consent gates: you are texting people who did not ask for this one message

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because a win-back text that breaks outreach rules is a liability, not a revenue play. Gate 1 — consent on file, opt-out in every message: SMS outreach needs consent captured at the job — on the signed work order, in plain language — and every text carries a working opt-out (reply STOP). One opt-out kills the whole sequence for that contact, not just one channel; a contact who opted out of texts never gets the follow-up email or the call either. Gate 2 — quiet hours and the do-not-contact list: no automated texts or calls before 8am or after 9pm in the contact's local time, ever — and you scrub every send against your own do-not-contact list before it fires. The sequence below is gated on these checks: a message that can't pass them doesn't go out. Gate 3 — the AI voice agent identifies itself: any AI call opens by saying it is calling on behalf of your company — it never impersonates a human employee, never bluffs a deadline, and hands any heat to a human in the same call. Every message also says who you are: the shop they paid two years ago, not a stranger with a discount. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice — run your consent language and your scripts past counsel for your state.

The short version

Our post-job review playbook (#6) works the jobs you just finished — this works the customers who went quiet months ago. And our maintenance-plan playbook (#8) enrolls completed-visit customers into plans and wins back lapsed plan members — this works the other 70–80% of the list: one-time-job customers, never-enrolled households, the people who paid you once and then drifted to whoever showed up first in their next Google search. The sequence is the dormancy ladder: a monthly CRM scan sorts quiet customers into warm / cooling / deep segments → a 21-day multi-channel win-back sequence (SMS, then email, then an AI check-in call, then a human call for high-value accounts) → bookings land straight on dispatch → the prevention loop makes sure next year's list doesn't go dormant the same way. Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The database is an asset, and specific beats generic

Rule 1 — clean the list before you touch it. A campaign fired at 30% dead numbers and bouncing emails doesn't just underperform — it torches your sender reputation and your SMS delivery. Scrub dead contacts, duplicates, and anyone on the do-not-contact list first. The sequence below assumes a clean list; hygiene is the price of admission, not step 6. Rule 2 — specific offers beat generic discounts. A text that says "your 2019 Goodman is coming up on its fall tune-up — $49, two spots this Thursday" books; "20% OFF ALL SERVICES!!!" gets deleted. Contractor campaigns that work all share this: the offer is tied to something already documented about the customer — their equipment, their last job, the season. Generic repair discounts, Crouch notes, get ignored because they feel like spam; specific ones feel like service. Both rules get printed and taped to the office wall — the sequence below never fires without them.

Step 1 — The dormancy trigger

1A monthly scan sorts the quiet into three buckets

The trigger is "days since last invoice," scanned monthly — not a gut feeling that "we should send something out." Contacts land in three segments: Warm (90–180 days quiet) — they're just busy, a nudge usually works; Cooling (180–365 days) — needs a real reason, a seasonal offer tied to their equipment; Deep (365+ days) — a re-introduction, with the strongest offer reserved for non-responders. The exclusion list matters as much as the segments: open disputes, one-star reviews, active maintenance-plan members (they have their own engine in playbook #8), recent estimates (that's playbook #4's job), and anyone who ever opted out. Deep-segment contacts get one full sequence a year — dormancy campaigns that never stop become the spam they were designed to replace. Make watches the CRM for contacts crossing the day thresholds and moves them into segments automatically, so the scan never depends on someone remembering to run it.

Runs on: your field-service CRM's "days since last invoice" + Make for the scan-and-segment logic.

Step 2 — The 21-day win-back sequence

2SMS opens, email explains, a voice closes

Day 0 — SMS. Short, specific, signed with your shop's name: "Hi Maria, it's Tom's HVAC — it's been 8 months since your AC tune-up. $49 fall tune-up spots this Thursday and Friday — reply YES and I'll hold one." Texts open at 90%+ for service businesses; email lands in promotions — the sequence opens with the channel that gets read. Day 4 — email. The longer version: their equipment, what the season demands of it, one clear booking link. Day 10 — the AI check-in call (step 3). Day 16 — second SMS, escalated offer — reserved for non-responders only; the offer starts conservative and increases only for people who didn't bite, which cuts discount expense versus flat-rate offers. Day 21 — human call for high-value accounts: commercial accounts and top-20% residential customers get a personal CSR call; everyone else cycles back into the seasonal queue. Stop rules are absolute: any booking → the sequence dies and the job lands on dispatch; any opt-out → the whole sequence dies across all channels; any complaint → a human takes over within the hour. And keep the first send capped — your office has to handle the reply volume, and callbacks inside the hour are what turn replies into booked jobs.

Runs on: Make (the 21-day sequence, segment timing, stop rules, dispatch handoff) + your CRM (service history personalization).

Step 3 — The AI check-in call

3Service, not a sales pitch — with the identity up front

The Day-10 call does five things in under two minutes: (1) identifies itself in the first sentence as calling on behalf of your company — never impersonating a human; (2) names the specific last job ("your water heater install last March"); (3) states one concrete seasonal offer tied to that history; (4) books on the call or texts the booking link while they're still on the line; (5) logs the outcome verbatim in the CRM — booked, call-back, not interested, do-not-contact. The script is trained on the tone from step 0: warm, specific, zero pressure, and it hands any heat to the owner in the same call. This call exists because a surprising share of dormancy is just drift — the customer liked you fine and simply forgot you exist. A real voice at Day 10 moves more of them than six more emails.

Runs on: Synthflow (the Day-10 check-in call) + ElevenLabs (voice quality for call realism) + Make (scheduling off the cooling/deep segments, logging outcomes to the CRM).

Step 4 — Measure booked jobs, not opens

4Tag every reply; the only metric is revenue

Every reply gets tagged back to the campaign, and every booked job gets its revenue attributed — that's the number that pays the bills, not opens or clicks. Report by segment: warm should convert best, cooling next, deep last. Expect the honest decay: reactivation response runs 15–25% for contacts dormant under six months and 5–10% for older ones in industry benchmarks (via MAXED AI's reactivation research, October 2026). The kill/keep rules are simple: if the warm segment books under 2%, the offer or the list hygiene is broken — fix those before touching the deep segment. If warm books 4%+ like the Relentless Digital case study, scale the sends and add the cooling segment. And once a year, run the reactivation math for the whole database: contacts × booking rate × average job value, minus campaign cost — that's the number you take to the bank, and it's the number that justifies doing this every month instead of every spring.

Runs on: your CRM (reply tagging, revenue attribution) + a monthly owner review of segment booking rates.

Step 5 — The prevention loop

5Stop manufacturing dormancy instead of just mining it

The sequence recovers revenue; the loop fixes the faucet. Three habits, all from elsewhere in this family: (1) the 48-hour post-job check-in text — "Hi Maria, it's Tom's HVAC, just making sure the AC is keeping up after yesterday's repair. Reply here if anything seems off." It's not a survey, it's a human-sounding check-in that opens a text thread you now own — a direct channel for every future offer. (2) Review capture (playbook #6) keeps the relationship warm while the job is fresh. (3) Maintenance-plan enrollment (playbook #8) is the structural fix — a member on auto-renewal never goes dormant at all. The honest hierarchy: the shop that runs #6 and #8 rarely needs #10. This playbook is the rescue net for the customers you already lost — run it monthly to recover them, run the loop so next year's rescue net catches less.

Runs on: Make (the 48-hour check-in, review-capture handoff) + playbook #8's enrollment engine for the structural fix.

Common questions

Won't win-back texts annoy my customers?

The opposite, usually — when they're specific. A text that names your shop, references their actual equipment, and offers one useful seasonal service reads as service, not spam. What annoys people is the fourth generic "20% OFF EVERYTHING" blast from a number they don't recognize. The consent gates up top are the whole guardrail: consent on the work order, opt-out in every message, one opt-out kills the entire sequence, quiet hours respected. Most homeowners welcome the reminder — they forgot you, not the other way around.

My list is old and messy. Is this still worth doing?

Worth doing after the hygiene pass, not before. Scrub dead numbers and bouncing emails first — a campaign to a dirty list torches deliverability and poisons the metrics. Then run the warm segment (90–180 days) first: it's the cheapest to win back and it proves the offer. Scale to cooling and deep only when the warm numbers say the offer works. And cap the first send so your office can handle the replies — a reply that waits two days for a callback is a customer you've now annoyed twice.

Email or SMS — which one actually books?

Both, in that order. SMS opens at 90%+ for service businesses while service email lands in promotions — so the sequence opens with the text and uses email for the longer, equipment-specific version. That's the whole Day-0/Day-4 structure. The voice call at Day 10 is what converts the people who read both and still didn't act.

Is the AI check-in call legal?

This page is not legal advice — but the design follows the conservative pattern: the agent identifies itself as calling on behalf of your company in the first sentence, it never impersonates a human, it never bluffs a deadline, consent for the outreach was captured on the signed work order with a working opt-out, and calls and texts respect quiet hours. Run your consent language and your scripts past counsel for your state before going live.

What about customers who left angry — bad reviews, disputes?

Excluded from every segment, automatically, in step 1. Reactivation is for drifted customers, not damaged ones. A win-back text to someone in an open dispute isn't marketing — it's a liability with a reply button. Keep that list separate, handle it by hand, and never let it near the sequence.
