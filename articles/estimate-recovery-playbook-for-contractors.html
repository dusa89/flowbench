The constraint: consent is captured at the quote — or this whole system stays off

AI voice calls to a customer's cell phone require prior express consent — that is the hard line. Unlike our speed-to-lead playbook, where the web lead submitted a form asking to be contacted, an estimate quote is not automatically consent to be called back four times over a month. Step 0 of this playbook is a single question at quote time, recorded in your CRM: "If you still have questions about your estimate, is it okay if we call you to check in?" One checkbox on the estimate form, timestamped and stored. No checkbox, no sequence — that customer gets email/SMS follow-up only, per whatever consent your existing channels already carry. Build the capture first; build the sequence second. Nothing here is compliance advice — verify the current TCPA rules and your state's requirements with counsel before you automate outbound voice.

The short version

The money is already sitting in your sent folder: the average contractor loses roughly $47,000 per year to estimates that go out and die in silence, and shops with structured follow-up close 43% more of them (fieldproxy.ai HVAC blueprint). The math on a real pipeline: 30 quotes a month at a $2,500 average, closed at 40% → 58% with a cadence in place, is about $13,500/month of recovered revenue from the same jobs you're already estimating (urbld.com, crawled October 2026). Five points of close rate on twenty $18,000 bids is $216,000/year riding on follow-up timing (builtwithjon). A 48-hour automated check-in alone recovers roughly 1 in 5 stalled bids (LinkedIn, September 2026), and one Texas electrician took close rate from 15% to 35% — $62,000/month recovered — on a 30-day follow-up sequence (forwardfirstmedia.com). This playbook prosecutes that silence over four touches: (0) consent captured at the quote, (1) a Day 1–2 check-in call, (2) a Day 5–7 objection-handling call, (3) a Day 14 re-engagement, and (4) a Day 30 win-back. The back end is Make (the sequence engine: triggers, spacing, CRM status sync, estimator alerts) plus Synthflow for the AI voice calls and ElevenLabs voices so the caller sounds like your office, not a robot. Unlike our contractor missed-call playbook (a ringing phone) and our speed-to-lead playbook (a web form), this one starts with an outbound dollar amount on a PDF that went dark — a different trigger, a different cadence, a different skill (objection handling, not speed). All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — Earn the right to call (at the quote)

0The consent capture

Before any automation exists, the estimate workflow gets one new field: a consent checkbox — "May we call you about your estimate if you have questions?" — presented verbally and in the estimate paperwork, timestamped, and stored against the customer record. The playbook's first objection is your own: most contractors say "I don't want to seem pushy." Reframe it the way customers experience it: a check-in two days after an estimate is service; three weeks of silence is what feels like nobody cared. The consent line makes that framing explicit — the customer asked you to follow up. Customers who decline still get the estimate, just without the voice sequence. This single gate is also what keeps the whole system defensible: every call in the sequence maps to a recorded yes.

Built with: a consent field added to your estimate/CRM form (JobNimbus, AccuLynx, ServiceTitan, or even a checkbox in your PDF template — whatever you already quote from) → Make reads it and either enrolls the estimate in the sequence or excludes it. Step 0 takes an afternoon and is the cheapest insurance in the playbook.

Step 1 — Day 1–2: the check-in call

1Did the estimate land?

Two days after the estimate goes out, the voice agent calls with one job: "Hi, this is [company]'s office — [estimator] sent over your estimate Tuesday. Did you get a chance to look at it, and is anything unclear?" That is it. No discount, no pressure, no closing — a service call that catches the estimate sitting in a spam folder, the line item nobody understood, and the customer who meant to read it and forgot. Unanswered calls get a short text with the estimator's name and a reply number. Every response (answered, voicemail, question raised) syncs to the estimate record so the estimator sees it before their next manual touch.

Built with: Make scenario — estimate sent (Day 0) → Day 1–2 timer → Synthflow outbound voice call (ElevenLabs voice) → outcome logged to CRM estimate record → estimator notification on any question or request. Keep the script to 60 seconds; the check-in is not the venue for the objection — that is step 2's job.

Step 2 — Day 5–7: the objection call

2Handle the stall out loud

By day 5 the estimate is a memory, and the customer's stall has a shape: they're price shopping, they need to talk to their spouse, or financing is the real blocker. The voice agent runs a short branching script — one branch per objection. Price shopping: "Totally fair — most of our customers compare two or three bids. Would it help if I walked through what's driving the number on yours?" Spouse: "Makes sense — want me to send a two-minute summary you can forward to them?" Financing: "We do have payment options — want the quick version?" Every branch ends the same way: book a human follow-up or send the summary, never a hard close on the call. The call log and the tagged objection land in the estimate record.

Built with: Synthflow script builder (three objection branches, tagged outcomes) → Make writes tags to the estimate record → summary-email or estimator callback task. The objection answers should be yours, not the AI's invention — feed it your estimator's actual one-liners. The branch library is the asset; keep it in one editable place.

Step 3 — Day 14: re-engagement

3The "still deciding?" touch

Two weeks out, the window is still open — most home-service buyers decide in the 2–4 week band. The re-engagement touch is lighter: a call plus a text offering a reason to reopen the conversation — a schedule slot opening up, a material price window, or simply "No rush — just wanted you to have our number if the other bids come in higher." The one thing this step must not be is a duplicate of step 2; it is a check on changed circumstances, not a re-argued estimate. Anyone still engaged but unbooked gets their timeline refreshed in the CRM (new decision date), which the sequence uses for step 4.

Built with: Make timer — Day 14 from estimate send (skip if booked or won) → Synthflow call + SMS fallback → CRM timeline field updated. Offer one concrete, time-bound reason per trade: roofers get shingle price windows; HVAC gets shoulder-season install slots; remodelers get the backlog opening.

Step 4 — Day 30: the win-back

4The long-stalled recovery

Day 30 is the last scheduled voice touch, and it earns its place: this is where the Texas electrician's $62,000/month number lives. The script acknowledges the gap honestly — "It's been a month since your estimate, and I wanted to check: did you go another direction, or is the project still on the table?" A real fraction of "went another direction" answers hide a bad install the customer now regrets; a real fraction of silence is a project that stalled on timing. Lost-to-competitor outcomes get logged with the reason (competitor name if offered — that is market research you used to pay for). Still-on-the-table outcomes get re-estimated if pricing expired, and booked directly. After day 30, the estimate leaves the voice sequence and enters long-term email nurture — the playbook hands off, it doesn't nag.

Built with: Make timer — Day 30 (skip if booked/won/lost) → Synthflow win-back call → Make routes outcome: re-estimate task, lost-reason logging, or email-nurture enrollment. Cap the sequence at four voice touches — five is where "follow-up" becomes a reputation problem.

Common objections, answered honestly

Won't four calls make us seem pushy?

Four calls in a month is normal commercial follow-up — what customers call pushy is irrelevant contact, not frequent contact. Step 1 is a service check, step 2 solves their actual stall, step 3 brings news, step 4 closes the loop honestly. Pushy is calling day 12 with nothing new to say — the sequence is designed so every touch has a job. And the step-0 consent line means the customer opted in to exactly this: "call me about my estimate."

We already do SMS follow-up — does voice add anything?

SMS platforms solved the consistency problem; the leak they leave is the objection problem. "I need to talk to my wife" over text gets "sounds good, let me know" and dies — on a voice call, the same objection gets a summary they can forward, a callback scheduled with both decision-makers, or a financing answer on the spot. If your quotes die on price-shopping and spouse-approval stalls, voice is the layer text can't reach. If your quotes die from never being sent on time, fix dispatch first — this playbook starts at "estimate sent."

Our estimates live in a quoting tool — will this integrate?

Honestly: this is the implementation load. Estimate data sits in JobNimbus, AccuLynx, ServiceTitan, spreadsheets, and paper pads — disconnected from everything. The Make back end needs the estimate's sent date, amount, customer phone, and consent flag. Budget a day of integration mapping before you write a single voice script; the sequence is trivial once the data flows. If your quoting tool has no API, the playbook still works with a shared sheet the estimator updates — ugly, but it ships.

Is the consent thing really required, or just lawyer paranoia?

Prior express consent for AI/prerecorded voice calls to cell phones is the settled federal baseline (TCPA), and states layer their own rules on top. "They asked for a quote" is not the same as "they agreed to be called four times." The step-0 capture is a 30-second conversation that makes the entire sequence defensible — skip it and you're building on sand. As with everything here: verify the current rules with counsel before automating outbound voice; this page is not compliance advice.
