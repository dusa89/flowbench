The credit-broker gates: the shop is not the lender — five gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before the first payment sheet leaves the shop. Gate 1: the shop never quotes a rate, APR, or monthly payment the lender has not confirmed in writing. "From $X a month" is only true when the math is real — the sheet carries the lender's compliant language verbatim, never your own paraphrase. Gate 2: financing gets offered to everyone, equally — never profile who "looks like they need it." Train every tech and comfort advisor to never make assumptions about what a customer can or cannot afford; steering the offer by appearance is how a sales ritual becomes a fair-lending problem. Gate 3: pre-qual is not approval. Nobody on your team promises approval or a rate — the lender's application decides, the shop presents options. Gate 4: the dealer fee lives in the pricebook, never in the margin. Same-as-cash and 0% promos charge the contractor 6-9% of the financed amount — either it's built into the financed price or the shop offers the cash alternative, never eaten silently. Gate 5: the script goes past an attorney once. Shops that touch consumer credit touch disclosure rules; one review of the payment sheet and the chase scripts costs less than one misquoted rate.

The verdict: finance the payment, not the price

Offer financing on every estimate over the shop's threshold (worked example below: $1,500) as a written 3-option payment sheet presented monthly-first, backed by a primary lender plus a second-look backup, with an automated chase on every unsigned proposal and the dealer fee priced into the book. The directional evidence (third-party, dated, below) says the payment framing lifts close rates and ticket sizes by double digits — but the vendor studies are directional. The verdict that counts is your own 90-day before/after on the 5-number scoreboard in Step 5.

1Stack the lenders: primary + second-look

Common third-party financing partners for home service contractors: Service Finance, GreenSky, Synchrony, and FTL Finance (per The Valley Marketing Group, Sep 2026). The dealer application takes a few days to process — start it this week, not the week the big estimate lands. Minimum viable stack is two lenders: the primary with the promotional terms you lead with, and a second-look program as the backup for declined applicants — ACCA data says second-look contractors finance 12% more sales. A decline currently ends the sale; after this step, a decline starts the backup application. Both applications are shop-side and $0.

Build path: dealer-direct applications, $0. No affiliate programs exist on the lender side for publishers — the lenders' programs are shop-side dealer agreements, so verify terms on each lender's own site.

2Price the dealer fee into the pricebook

Same-as-cash and 0% promos charge the contractor 6-9% of the financed amount — $312-$468 on a $5,200 AC replacement. Gate 4's rule: this is a line in the pricebook, never a silent margin leak. Two honest ways to carry it: build it into the financed price (playbook #24, the flat-rate pricebook, is where the loaded-labor math already lives — the dealer fee joins it), or offer the cash alternative: one trade-press source recommends 5% cash back for customers who pay cash (Contracting Business, Sep 2026). Run the fee per job type, not per job — promo fees make sense on $8,000 system replacements and eat the margin on $600 repairs, which is why Step 3 has a threshold.

Cross-link: Playbook #24 (flat-rate pricebook) — the dealer fee is a pricebook input, not a post-hoc surprise.

3Build the 3-option payment sheet

Every estimate over the shop's threshold (worked example: $1,500) leaves with exactly 3 written options: pay in full, promotional terms, standard monthly. The presentation is monthly-first — lead with the payment, never the lump sum: "we can do this for $X a month — how does that sound?" (Contracting Business, Sep 2026). 97% of home improvement customers want to know about financing on a large-ticket item (Synchrony via Contracting Business, Sep 2026) — so the sheet answers the question they're already asking. One page, only lender-confirmed numbers (Gate 1), the lender's compliant language verbatim. When the customer picks the promotional tier over the base model — and the ACHR survey says always-offer shops sell fewer base models — the upgrade paid for the dealer fee.

4Run the 100%-offer ritual — and the automated chase

The ritual: financing is offered on 100% of qualifying estimates, by every tech and comfort advisor, every time. Never profile who "looks like they need it" — train the team to never make assumptions about what a customer can afford (Contracting Business, Sep 2026). Put it in marketing too: financing callouts in ads and a "from $X/month" line on service pages are customer courtesy and expectation now, not a gimmick (The Valley Marketing Group, Sep 2026). Then automate the follow-through: every unsigned proposal over threshold gets the payment-options link at 24 hours and 72 hours (a Make scenario off your CRM's proposal status — affiliate placeholder), and an AI voice agent places the 48-hour check-in call on big-ticket estimates (Synthflow + ElevenLabs — affiliate placeholders): "the payment options on your estimate are still open — any questions?" Never a hard close; always an open door. The abandoned application gets the same chase — pre-qual drop-off is the leak this step plugs.

Build path: Make 35%/12mo + Synthflow 30%/15mo + ElevenLabs 22%/12mo (program terms verified Oct 1-2, 2026) — affiliate placeholders, confirm before buying. Cross-link: Playbook #4 (estimate recovery) is the rescue layer under this chase; Playbook #11 (post-job upsell) — monthly framing lifts attach rates on the add-on ticket too.

5Keep the monthly financing scoreboard

Five numbers, reviewed monthly: (1) offer rate — estimates that got the sheet vs estimates that qualified (target: 100%; anything less is a training problem, not a market problem); (2) pre-qual rate; (3) financed close rate vs cash close rate; (4) financed average ticket vs cash average ticket; (5) decline-rescue rate — second-look saves as a share of primary declines. Compare against the shop's own 90-day baseline from before the ritual — the vendor studies above are the directional case; the shop's own before/after is the verdict. If financed close rate doesn't beat cash close rate by month 3, the ritual is broken (usually: the sheet isn't leaving with every estimate, or the chase isn't firing) — not the concept.

Honest weak points

(1) The demand bench is vendor-adjacent. The strongest numbers on this page — the 3X spend gap, the 8-11% revenue growth — come from a financing platform's own Q2 2026 report. They sell the thing they measure. Treat every figure as directional; the shop's own 90-day before/after is the real number. (2) Dealer fees are real money. 6-9% on promo programs can erase the ticket lift on thin-margin work — which is why Step 2 exists and why the threshold exists. (3) The application funnel leaks. Customers start pre-qual and abandon; the 48-hour chase recovers some, never all. (4) The 2018 EnerBank figure is the floor, not the ceiling — a 2018 executive citing internal studies via a trade article; the 2025-2026 bench supersedes it. (5) The compliance edge is the product, not the appendix. A shop that misquotes a rate or steers the offer by profile is in credit-broker territory — the five gates are load-bearing.

Questions shops actually ask

How much does offering financing cost the shop?

$0 to set up the dealer programs. The cost is the 6-9% dealer fee on promotional programs, carried in the pricebook (Step 2) — and the 5% cash-back alternative keeps the cash customer honest. The fee only hurts when it's a surprise; priced in, it's a customer-acquisition cost with a measured return.

When in the estimate do we bring it up?

Lead with it — present the 3-option payment sheet with the estimate, never after the price objection (Contracting Business, Sep 2026). Financing offered as an objection-handler feels like a rescue; financing presented with the options feels like the shop's normal way of doing business. The 100%-offer ritual in Step 4 only works when it's the default, not the exception.

Do we really need a second-look lender?

If declines currently end the sale, yes — ACCA's figure is 12% more financed sales for shops with second-look programs (cited Sep 2026). The backup application is the cheapest revenue on this page: the customer already said yes to the job, they just failed the first lender's screen.

Does this work for small repair tickets?

Not automatically — the worked threshold is $1,500 because the dealer fee eats thin-margin small tickets. Run the fee per job type (Step 2): system replacements and full re-pipes finance beautifully; $200 drain calls don't. The threshold is the shop's call; the math per type is non-negotiable.

How is this different from the estimate-recovery and post-job upsell playbooks?

#4 (estimate recovery) chases quotes already lost — its object is the lost quote, its trigger is silence after 48 hours, its metric is recovered estimates. #11 (post-job upsell) adds scope to a visit already won — its object is the add-on ticket, its trigger is the tech on site, its metric is attach rate. This playbook changes how the customer pays for the big ticket before the yes/no decision — its object is the payment sheet, its trigger is every qualifying estimate, its metric is financed close rate. Same revenue family, different moment, different job.
