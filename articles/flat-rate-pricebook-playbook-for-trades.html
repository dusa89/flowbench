The discipline gates: a pricebook is a loaded cost + a margin target — five gates, non-negotiable

Every pricing consultant in the trade sells the same cure in a different wrapper. Read the gates before you price a single task. Gate 1: flat rate done wrong loses more than time and materials. A pricebook built on an unloaded labor rate is a machine for losing exactly the same amount on every job — faster and more consistently than before. This playbook has a fork: shops still on time-and-materials complete Step 1 (the loaded rate) before anything else, because a flat rate multiplied by a wrong rate is the most expensive mistake in the book. Gate 2: the "90% lose money" figure is consultant consensus, not a study. HVAC consultant Bill Ligon wrote (Contracting Business, evergreen) that in his experience nearly 90% of contractors lose money in their service departments — and that every consultant he has spoken with shares the observation. It is a repeated industry observation, not measured data. The measured data — 3% to 8% net margins, 7% vs 4% flat-rate vs other — is the floor. Gate 3: The New Flat Rate is a vendor selling this. Their published pricing ($595 for one section or $995 for three, plus $75/month per user with updates and app, per Contracting Business coverage) is a buy-option benchmark, not a neutral recommendation — it appears in the build-vs-buy table below as the vendor option, labeled. Gate 4: margin is not markup, and the confusion is the industry's canonical math error. A 30% margin requires a 42.9% markup on cost — pricing a $100-cost task at $130 delivers a 23% margin, not 30% (ProBuilder, 2024, margin-vs-markup). Every price in this book is checked margin-up, not markup-down, and the dispatcher never "marks up 30%" as a substitute for pricing to a 30% margin. Gate 5: this is a pricing lane, not an accounting lane. The loaded-rate build in Step 1 changes how jobs are quoted but not how revenue is recognized — run the finished numbers past your accountant once, the first time, before the annual reset becomes habit.

The verdict: the pricebook is the shop's profit engine — and most are running last decade's numbers

Contractors using flat-rate pricing reported an average net profit of 7%, compared with 4% among those using other methods (Contracting Business, reporting the ACCA Contractor of the Future study, December 2025). That 3-point gap is the whole game in an industry where most HVAC contractors operate on 3% to 8% net margins (neuron.expert, September 2026). The gap opens at the labor rate: a technician paid $30 an hour actually costs $42 to $48 an hour once payroll taxes, workers' comp, and benefits land — a 40% to 60% increase over base wage (same source). Price the job at $30 and the clock is running against you from the first minute: a $25-per-hour underbilling gap across a tech's 1,500 billable hours a year is $37,500 in lost profit per tech, per year (neuron.expert, September 2026). This playbook builds the pricebook in five steps: cost your real loaded labor rate, install the margin-vs-markup discipline, flat-rate the 20 tasks that carry 80% of revenue, run the annual reset ritual so last year's costs stop pricing this year's jobs, and hold the book at the door with a price-confidence script — because a pricebook nobody defends is a suggestion.

1Build your loaded labor rate — the audit (Week 1)

Pull 3 numbers and do the arithmetic once. (a) The true hourly cost per tech: base wage × 1.4 to 1.6 for payroll taxes, workers' comp, and benefits (the 40–60% burden, neuron.expert, September 2026). A $30 wage becomes $42 to $48 before the van ever moves. (b) Overhead per billable hour: total annual overhead (rent, office, insurance, software, vehicle fixed costs) divided by total annual billable hours — not total hours, billable hours. (c) The target net margin: CFMA's Construction Financial Benchmarker put specialty trades at 22.4% gross and 7.7% net before tax for fiscal 2024, with the best firms at 14.2% and firms under $10 million in revenue worst at 6.5% (via Simpro, September 2026) — price toward the top quartile, not the average. Add (a) + (b), then divide by (1 − target margin) — never multiply by (1 + margin), per Gate 4. The output is one number per tech level. Post it where estimates are written. Time-and-materials shops: this number IS the conversion — multiply it into every quoted hour before any flat-rate task price exists.

Tools: the shop's existing sheet. One page, 3 inputs, 1 number. No software to buy — the gap this closes (up to $37,500/year per tech) is arithmetic, not tooling.

2Install the margin-vs-markup discipline (Week 1)

Margin is profit as a share of price; markup is profit as a share of cost — and mixing them is the mistake that quietly shaves points off every ticket (ProBuilder, 2024). The rule is a posted cheat sheet, not a lecture: to land a 30% margin, multiply cost by 1.429; for 40%, multiply by 1.667; for 50%, multiply by 2.0. The second rule kills the discount pricebook: never let a discount live in the book itself. The pricebook prices to the target margin; any discount is a one-line, one-time entry on the invoice with a reason code — because a permanent 10% discount in the book is a permanent margin surrender, and the fractional-CFO case is that the best-run HVAC, plumbing, and electrical shops hold 20% net while most keep 5% to 12% (Profitability Partners via Service Business Mastery, 2026). Teach the cheat sheet to dispatchers and techs, not just the owner — the person quoting the job is the person who needs the math.

Tools: a laminated card at the dispatch desk. Cost: $0.

3Flat-rate the top 20 tasks — 80% of revenue (Weeks 2–3)

Run last year's completed jobs through a sort: the 20 most-performed tasks carry the bulk of revenue — capacitor swaps, drain cleanings, tune-ups, faucet installs, the median-ticket work like the $269 AC capacitor and $299 drain cleaning medians (Home Therapist Tampa invoice study, globenewswire, July 2026). Each task gets one price: loaded labor hours (Step 1's rate × timed task hours, not estimated) + parts at current cost + overhead allocation + target margin. Time the tasks from actual job history, not memory — memory quotes the fast day. Offer tiers where the work has them (repair vs rebuild vs replace menus — the "section selection" pattern vendors sell at $595/section, per Contracting Business): tiers raise the average ticket while removing selling pressure from the tech, because the customer picks the level, not the price. Flat-rate converts against the stopwatch problem too — techs perform better when they are not racing a clock while a customer watches (Contractor Magazine, evergreen), which feeds the upsell attaches in #11's playbook.

Tools: the sorted job export + the sheet from Step 1. A Make scenario that turns the sheet into the field-ready price menu is the optional automation layer (Make verified against its own affiliate and pricing pages October 1–2, 2026).

4The annual reset ritual — every January, never ad hoc (ongoing)

A pricebook decays at the speed of input costs. The Associated General Contractors found nonresidential input costs climbed 7.1% year over year in June 2026 while bid prices rose only 3.5% (via Simpro, September 2026) — that 3.6-point gap is exactly what a stale pricebook absorbs silently. The ritual, 4 hours every January: (1) update parts costs against the last 90 days of purchase history (#23's van-stock sheet is the source); (2) update loaded labor rates against current wages and benefit costs; (3) add the year's training investment back in — Nexstar's rule is that pricing must be adjusted periodically to compensate for training time that could have been sold on the jobsite (Contractor Magazine); (4) re-run the margin-vs-markup check on the top 20 tasks; (5) retire tasks nobody performed. Never reprice ad hoc mid-year except on a parts-cost spike — constant tinkering teaches techs the book is negotiable, and a negotiable book is a suggestion (see Step 5).

Tools: the same sheet, one calendar invite in January. The buy alternative is a vendor-maintained book with updates (The New Flat Rate at $75/month per user — vendor price, labeled).

5Hold the book at the door — the price-confidence script (ongoing)

The best pricebook in the trade fails at the kitchen table if the price comes out as a question. The script has 4 beats: (1) name the price once, flatly, with the task scope attached — "the capacitor replacement is $269, parts and labor, guaranteed"; (2) present the tier, not the discount — "there's also the rebuild option at $X if you'd rather"; (3) on pushback, defend the book, not the number — "that's our book price for this repair, same for every customer"; (4) never improvise a discount on the spot — any exception goes through the owner with a reason code (Step 2's rule). Pember Research's 2026 finding is the tailwind: flat-rate providers receive fewer negative reviews citing unexpected charges (September 2026) — the book is a trust instrument, not just a margin one. Track the quote close rate weekly: when it dips, the fix is the script and the tiers, not the prices — because the #4 estimate-recovery playbook can only win back quotes that were worth winning, and the pricebook decides that.

Tools: the 4-beat card on every tech's phone. Role-play it once, monthly.

Questions this page must answer

We're still on time-and-materials. Can we use this?

Yes — start at Step 1 and stop there until the loaded rate is posted. The loaded labor rate is the conversion: multiply it into every quoted hour and your T&M pricing is already margin-aware. Build the 20-task flat-rate book (Step 3) as the second project, once quoting at the loaded rate is habit.

What's the difference between margin and markup, really?

Margin is profit divided by price; markup is profit divided by cost. A $130 price on $100 of cost is a 30% markup but only a 23% margin — to hold a 30% margin on $100 of cost you need $142.86 (a 42.9% markup). The posted cheat sheet in Step 2 keeps the whole team on the margin side (ProBuilder, 2024).

How often should we actually update the book?

Once a year, every January — parts, wages, training time, overhead — with one exception: a parts cost spike gets a mid-year adjustment on that task line only. Input costs rose 7.1% year over year in June 2026 while bid prices rose 3.5% (AGC via Simpro, September 2026); shops that skip the ritual eat that gap silently.

Should we buy a vendor menu-pricing system instead?

Buy the system when the DIY book is already running and you want field app automation — not as the way to learn the math. The vendor's published pricing is $595 for one section or $995 for three, plus $75/month per user with updates and support (Contracting Business coverage — verify current pricing on the vendor's site). The discipline in Steps 1, 2, and 5 is identical either way.

What if customers push back on the flat price?

Flat-rate providers receive fewer negative reviews citing unexpected charges (Pember Research, September 2026) — the certainty is the product. On pushback, Step 5's script defends the book ("our book price, same for every customer") and offers the tier, not a discount. Discounts live on the invoice with a reason code, never in the book.
