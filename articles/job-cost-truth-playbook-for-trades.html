The Job-Cost Truth Playbook for Trades

Playbook #34 — FlowBench field playbooks. Stats are third-party and dated, not guarantees. Verify terms on vendor sites before buying.

Independent, not accounting advice. This playbook describes a measurement process for trade shops — your CPA owns your chart of accounts and your books. Every figure on this page is third-party and dated — direction, not a promise for your market.

The constraints: measurement before motivation — read these before step 0

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because job-costing is the one playbook that fails silently when the inputs lie. Gate 1 — garbage in, garbage out: the system is only as honest as the daily time entry behind it. If crews clock "shop" for everything and the office sorts it out later, there is no playbook here — there is fiction. Daily coding to job and cost code is the non-negotiable. Gate 2 — the estimate must be comparable: step 0 breaks the bid into the same cost codes the field reports against. An estimate that says "labor: $4,000" cannot be compared to field time coded to "rough-in / trim / service" — the comparison needs the same buckets on both sides. Gate 3 — reviews fix estimating, not crews: the post-job review compares estimate to actual so the next bid gets better. It is a pricing process, not a performance process — the moment crews think the flash report is a scorecard, the time entries become political and the data dies. Run the cost-code structure and the review cadence past your accountant once; then run the playbook.

The short version

Most trade shops are flying half blind on the only question that matters: which jobs made money and which ones lost it. An August 2026 study by CMiC and Dodge Construction Network found that fewer than half of contractors (41%) can identify cost overruns while they are still manageable on even half of their projects, and only 6% have a fully connected project-controls environment (GlobeNewswire, August 5, 2026 — vendor-commissioned study, so read it as directional, but the gap it names is the gap every practitioner recognizes). The 2026 Barnes Dennig contractor compensation survey names the pressure behind it: labor costs are a top operational issue for 67% of contractors, and margin pressure from competition for 48% — and labor is exactly the cost that leaks when it isn't tracked to the job. The practitioner's version is blunter: without job-level financial visibility updated through the month, you know which jobs are running but not "which ones are tracking above margin, which are tracking below" (constructioncfo.net, The Construction CFO), and as the EdgeStrat Finance job-cost health check puts it — if field labor isn't consistently coded to the correct job, "job profitability becomes guesswork." The job of this playbook is to end the guessing: budget the job in cost codes before mobilization → code every labor hour and every receipt to the job, daily → flash the estimated-vs-actual margin every Friday → review the closed job → feed the actuals back into the next estimate. The build is Make (the Friday flash: timesheets + invoices + the estimate, aggregated into one margin-per-job report with at-risk flags), Synthflow (the foreman's end-of-day voice log — 30 seconds of spoken day-totals coded to job and cost code, for crews who hate apps), ElevenLabs voices so the check-in call sounds like the office, and a free-tier time-tracking app for the raw clock-ins. All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — The job budget before mobilization

0Break the bid into cost codes — the same buckets the field reports

Nothing in this playbook works until the estimate speaks the same language as the field. So every job gets set up before a dollar is spent: the contract value, then a budget in simple cost codes — labor (by phase: rough-in, trim, service), materials, subcontractors, equipment, permits. The EdgeStrat Finance job-cost health check frames the test: "Every job starts with a budget tied to cost codes. If this is No, overruns stay hidden until the end." Keep the code list short — 6 to 10 buckets, plain words the crew understands. A cost-code structure "clearly understood" by the field is one of the same health check's gates, because a confused crew codes everything to "miscellaneous" and the system records history instead of finding problems. One owner sets up the job budget in the system before mobilization — same week the bid is signed.

Runs on: your estimating tool's cost-code output + 10 minutes of setup per job in your books.

Step 1 — Labor coded to the job, daily

1Every hour lands on a job and a cost code the same day it is worked

Labor is the biggest cost and the leakiest bucket — so it gets the strictest rule: every crew hour is assigned to a job and a cost code the day it is worked. The field mechanic that survives contact with real crews is the 30-second end-of-day voice log: the foreman gets an AI voice call at 4:30 PM, says who worked where on what (job + cost code), and the totals land in the tracker coded — no app the crew has to love, no paperwork at the kitchen table. GPS-verified clock-in apps (free tiers exist for small crews) are the buy option for shops that want the raw time records without the call — vendors like TaskTag sell exactly this (cost-code attribution at clock-in, per their own September 2026 write-up; vendor numbers, read as ceiling). The discipline rule is the whole game: "Labor is assigned to jobs weekly or more often. If this is No, labor problems surface too late to correct" (EdgeStrat Finance). Friday-late time sheets are not a time sheet — they are a memory exercise. Enforce daily; automate the nag with Make.

Runs on: Synthflow + ElevenLabs for the foreman voice log; Make for the missing-entry nag; a free-tier time-tracking app as the buy option.

Step 2 — Materials and subs tied to the job

2No receipt, no sub invoice, no equipment rental drifts into "general"

Labor truth dies if the other half of the cost floats free. So every material purchase, every sub invoice, every rental gets tagged to the job at the point of entry — the supply-house receipt is photographed and coded the day it is bought, not at month-end. Subcontractor costs get tied to the job specifically, "not to a general expense category" (primeentrybookkeeping.com's job profitability review, which exists as a post-job checklist for exactly this). The office rule is binary: an untagged cost gets coded before it gets booked. Week one is painful; week four is habit.

Runs on: your books' job-tagging (every mainstream accounting tool has it) + a receipt-photo inbox the office clears daily.

Step 3 — The Friday flash

3Estimated vs actual, per job, every Friday — the at-risk jobs flag themselves

This is the step the 41% from the CMiC/Dodge study never built: a weekly margin report that lands while there is still time to act. Every Friday, Make pulls the week's coded labor hours, the tagged materials and sub costs, and the step-0 budget, and produces one page per active job: estimated cost vs actual-to-date, margin consumed vs percent complete, and a flag on any job where labor is burning faster than progress. A job at 40% complete with 60% of its labor budget spent is not "running a little hot" — it is a margin fire with a date on it, and the flash names it while the crew is still on site. The owner reads it in 15 minutes on Friday afternoon; Monday's schedule gets adjusted from the flags, not from gut feel. This is the automation leg that makes the whole playbook passive after setup — the discipline is steps 0–2, the payoff is this.

Runs on: Make for the Friday aggregation + flags; your books as the cost source of truth.

Step 4 — The post-job review

4Close the job by asking: did the estimate survive contact with the job?

When a job closes, the revenue is visible — "what's harder to see is whether the margin held, and if it didn't, where it went" (primeentrybookkeeping.com). So every significant job gets a 30- minute review inside two weeks of closeout, before memory smooths the details: estimated vs actual labor by phase, material variance, sub cost variance, the callback and change-order events (playbooks #25 and #26 own those motions; this review harvests their cost impact). The review answers four questions — were all hours recorded and assigned, did any hours hide in overhead, where did the estimate miss, and what was the actual labor cost per hour delivered on this job. Write it down in one page. File it with the job.

Runs on: a one-page review template + the Friday flashes from the job's life; 30 minutes inside 2 weeks of closeout.

Step 5 — The estimate-feedback loop

5Actuals become the next bid — the pricing gets smarter every quarter

The review is worthless as a document and priceless as a feed. Every quarter, the estimator sits with the review file and revises the bid templates: the phases that consistently overrun get more hours, the winners get held, the "actual labor cost per hour delivered" becomes the loaded rate the next bid uses instead of the rate the owner wishes were true. This is the compound leg of the whole playbook — and it is what makes this different from every money-in playbook in the family (below). The 6%-connected-shops from the CMiC/Dodge study are connected precisely because their estimating and their field data talk to each other. Yours do too, now — on a quarterly cadence the estimator owns.

Runs on: the review file + the bid template; Make can stage the quarterly actuals-vs-estimate rollup automatically.

Questions shops actually ask

We already have accounting software with job costing. Why this playbook?

Because the CMiC/Dodge August 2026 study says 94% of contractors lack fully connected project controls — the software is usually there and the visibility isn't. The playbook isn't a new ledger; it's the discipline loop (daily coding, weekly flash, post-job review, quarterly estimate feed) that makes the software you already pay for tell you the truth while there's still time to act.

My crews will never fill out timesheets properly.

That's exactly why step 1 leads with the foreman voice log instead of an app — one 30-second call where the foreman says who worked where on what, coded by automation. Gate 1 is honest about this: if the input lies, the system is fiction. Start with the lowest-friction capture that still codes to job and cost code daily.

How many cost codes do we need?

Six to ten, in words the crew already uses. The failure mode is 40 codes nobody understands (everything goes to "miscellaneous") — and the success mode is a short list where the estimate buckets and the field buckets are the same list. Your accountant blesses the list once.

When does this pay for itself?

First at-risk flag in step 3 — one labor overrun caught mid-job usually funds the whole system. Then quarterly, when step 5's actuals stop the estimate from repeating the same miss. There are no vendor promises on this page; the payoff is the discipline, and the discipline is yours.

Isn't this just job costing? Every contractor knows job costing.

Every contractor knows of job costing — 41% can catch overruns while they're manageable on even half their projects (CMiC/Dodge, August 2026). Knowing the concept and running the loop are different things. This playbook is the loop: budget, code, flash, review, feed back — every week, every job.
