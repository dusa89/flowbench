The honesty gates: proof, not performance — brand-building, not a lead machine

This playbook turns your real work into the content that makes you the familiar name — it is not a lead machine, and the page that pretends otherwise is selling you the agency retainer. Five gates, printed before the verdict, non-negotiable. Gate 1: social is brand-building first, sales second. A home-services marketing strategist interviewed by Contracting Business puts it plainly: engagement measures attention, not intent — "a homeowner can like a funny post without ever needing a new roof"; in the trades, "familiarity beats flashy." Expect trust and recall, not jobs from the first post. Gate 2: film only with the customer's OK. A photo/video release rides on the work order before any camera comes out. Never film kids, addresses or house numbers, license plates, or anything inside the home beyond what the release covers. Gate 3: the tech must actually film — that is the whole failure mode. No pipeline survives a crew that will not hold the phone for 90 seconds at the end of the job. The ritual in Step 1 is the playbook; the software is the amplifier. Gate 4: AI edits, a human approves. Nothing posts without an owner or lead-tech review. AI never fabricates jobsite footage — a synthetic "before/after" is a trust-killing lie in a trade that sells in person. Gate 5: the big survey numbers are self-reported. Wyzowl's 2026 figures come from a survey of video marketers, not audited revenue — they set the ceiling, not the promise, and they are labeled that way below.

The verdict: every job feeds the engine; familiarity wins the call

The homeowner's buying moment for a trade is rare and urgent — a failed AC, a leaking water heater — and at that moment they call the name they already know. A 45-to-90-second narrated walkthrough of a job you completed, posted two to four times a week, is how you become that name. One home-service marketing guide calls the narrated job video "your strongest lead generator" for home-service companies — problem, what you did, what the result looks like (Biondo Creative, September 2026). And Wyzowl's 2026 survey finds 63% of video marketers now use AI tools to create or edit their videos, up from 51% a year earlier — the editing bottleneck that used to cost $800 to $3,000 a month for a short-form editor is exactly what the AI pipeline in this playbook replaces. The machine: a 90-second end-of-job filming ritual, a weekly footage dump, an AI rough-cut pipeline with human approval, and a posting rhythm of 2 to 4 times a week across Google Business Profile, TikTok, Reels, and Shorts.

Why this works as a build: Wyzowl's 2026 report finds the two biggest reasons businesses do NOT use video are "they don't think they need it" and "it's too expensive," with lack of time close behind. A trade shop has the opposite assets of the average survey respondent — the content walks through your door every single day, and your phone already films in 4K. The expensive part was never the camera; it was the editing and the posting discipline. This playbook automates both for the price of the AI pipeline.

Step 0 — the two rules

0The two rules

Rule 1: the phone is enough. No crew, no cinema camera, no shoot day. The customer's trust comes from seeing real work, real people, real jobs — a trade strategist's checklist says "real work, real people, and real community" beat flashy production (Contracting Business, 2026). 4K phone footage is the production value; the editing pipeline supplies the polish.

Rule 2: cadence beats virality. Two to four posts a week, every week — "consistency isn't about posting every day; it's about predictability" (Contracting Business, 2026). A shop that posts three videos a week for six months has a 78-video library; a shop chasing one viral hit has nothing. The engine's job is to make the cadence effortless, not to manufacture virality.

Tools: the company phone + the release line on the work order (Gate 2).

Step 1 — the 90-second end-of-job ritual

1The 90-second end-of-job ritual

Before the tech packs the tools, they shoot three shots, fifteen to thirty seconds each: (1) the wide before — the dirty condenser, the rusted water heater, the cracked driveway; (2) the problem close-up — whatever the customer pointed at, narrated in one sentence ("this blower motor was drawing 9 amps on a 6-amp rated motor — that's why it was humming"); (3) the finished reveal — the clean install, the restored surface. One sentence of narration per shot, spoken to the camera like a person, not a commercial. The reveal format is "one of the most watched video types on social media" (Biondo Creative, September 2026) — power washing, cleaning, painting, and flooring shops have a built-in reveal on every single job.

Tools: company phone (landscape for GBP/YouTube; the pipeline crops vertical for TikTok/Reels in Step 3). Storage: the weekly dump folder in Step 2 — never the tech's camera roll.

Step 2 — the weekly footage dump

2The weekly footage dump

Every Friday the techs upload the week's three-shot clips to one shared drive folder with a naming convention the automation can read: JOBID-before.mp4, JOBID-problem.mp4, JOBID-after.mp4. An automation watches the folder, pairs the triplets by job ID, drops the pairs into a review queue with the job's trade and service type attached from the dispatch board, and flags orphan clips (an after with no before) back to the tech's phone. Five jobs a week at three shots each is the raw material for 2 to 4 finished videos — nothing here costs a tech more than the 90 seconds they already spent in Step 1.

Tools: a shared drive folder + a Make scenario (verified affiliate leg, make.com/en/affiliate, checked Oct 1–2, 2026): watch-folder trigger → pair triplets → log to a review sheet → orphan alerts. No footage ever leaves the shop's own storage until a human approves the edit (Step 3).

Step 3 — the AI rough-cut pipeline (AI drafts, a human approves)

3The AI rough-cut pipeline

Each paired triplet enters the edit pipeline automatically: the AI assembles the three shots in order, cuts the hook first (the reveal shot as the opening frame — the satisfying moment up front, then the story), burns in captions (most short-form video is watched muted), and generates the 60-to-90-second narration track from the tech's one-sentence notes using a consistent shop voice. The owner or lead tech gets a review queue every Monday: approve, trim, or reject in one tap. Approved cuts export in two masters — a 30-second GBP/vertical cut and a 60-to-90-second TikTok/Reels/Shorts cut. Rejected cuts go back with one-line feedback the pipeline remembers. Gate 4 lives here: the AI edits real footage; it never synthesizes jobsite scenes, before/afters, or testimonials.

Tools: Make scenario (verified) for the queue logic; ElevenLabs (verified affiliate leg, elevenlabs.io/affiliates-terms, checked Oct 1–2, 2026) for the consistent narration voice; an AI editing tool from the short-form bench (Submagic 30% lifetime recurring / CapCut up to 35% recurring tiered — both verified official Oct 1, 2026 — our ClipStack lane tracks the full comparison) for captions and rough cuts. Roughly: the pipeline replaces the $800–$3,000/month editor retainer (hirevire.com, 2026).

Step 4 — the posting rhythm (2–4 a week, everywhere the customer looks)

4The posting rhythm

Approved videos queue in a scheduler on a fixed rhythm: Google Business Profile first — this is where the buying intent already sits, and listings with visuals get 42% more direction requests and 35% more website clicks (Google, via whitespark.ca) — then TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts on the same day. GBP takes the 30-second cut (the platform's maximum); the social platforms take the 60-to-90-second cut. Every post carries the same three elements: the job type and neighborhood in the caption ("tankless water heater install, Summerlin — the utility closet was 24 inches wide, here's how we made it work"), the service area in the hashtags or tags, and the booking link. One scheduler, one calendar, all four surfaces — the discipline of Step 0's Rule 2, automated.

Tools: a scheduler from the social-scheduling bench (Metricool 25%-lifetime/50% tiers, SocialBee 20% recurring — both verified official Oct 1–2, 2026; our SchedStack lane tracks the full comparison) for the multi-surface queue; Make (verified) to feed approved cuts from the review sheet to the scheduler automatically.

Step 5 — the feedback loop (measure what matters, ignore the rest)

5The feedback loop

Monthly, the owner checks three numbers and ignores everything else: (1) GBP Insights — profile views, direction requests, and calls from the profile, month over month since the engine started; (2) branded search — how often the shop's own name is searched (the familiarity metric the whole playbook is built on); (3) the human signal — how many new customers say "I saw your video" on the booking call. Likes, shares, and follower counts are explicitly NOT the metric: "engagement metrics measure attention, not intent" (Contracting Business, 2026). If GBP calls rise and the human signal appears, the engine stays. If after six months neither moves, kill the cadence before blaming the content — the failure is usually Step 1's ritual (techs stopped filming), not Step 3's editing.

Tools: GBP Insights (free, in the GBP dashboard); a monthly Make scenario that pulls the post performance and the booking-call source notes into one sheet.

Step 6 — the compound (one job, five assets)

6The compound

The engine's real output is not one video — it is the asset stack every job now produces automatically: (1) the GBP video that feeds the profile Google rewards with calls; (2) the TikTok/Reel/Short that feeds reach; (3) the before/after stills pulled from the footage, which feed the review page — pair them with the review-capture system in playbook #6 and the five-star review now arrives with photographic proof; (4) the FAQ cut, embedded on the service page, which "can generate calls for years" (Biondo Creative, September 2026); (5) the job-story text from the narration script, which becomes the next Google Post. This is the family compound at full power: the review engine (#6) supplies the trust, the video engine supplies the proof, the scheduler lane (SchedStack) supplies the rhythm, and the editing-tool lane (ClipStack) supplies the bench — four lanes, one job's footage.

Tools: the same three-shot triplet from Step 1, now earning five times. The stills export comes from the editing bench; the FAQ cut reuses Step 3's pipeline with the FAQ script template.

Questions this page must answer

Is social media really worth it if it doesn't produce direct leads?

Read Gate 1: for most contractors, social is brand-building first and a sales tool second. The honest promise is familiarity — when the furnace dies, the homeowner calls the name they've been seeing in their feed for six months. That is exactly why the feedback loop in Step 5 measures GBP calls, branded search, and the "I saw your video" signal — not likes.

How much time does this take the tech each week?

Ninety seconds per job, at the end of the job: three shots, one sentence each. Five jobs a week is under eight minutes of filming. The Friday dump is a phone upload. Everything after that — pairing, rough cuts, scheduling — is the automation. The shop's total human load is the owner's Monday review queue: approve, trim, or reject.

What if the owner and the techs are camera-shy?

The camera points at the work, not the person — the wide before, the problem close-up, the finished reveal. The narration can be the consistent AI shop voice from Step 3 rather than a person on camera. The Climate Experts story (one truck to $15M) was founder-led, but the format that works for shy shops is the job-story video with no faces at all: problem, work, result.

Can AI-generated video replace filming real jobs?

No — and the playbook bans it. A trade sells in person, at the customer's door; synthetic before/afters or fabricated job footage would destroy the exact trust the engine builds. Gate 4: the AI edits real footage; it never synthesizes it. If a clip can't be filmed (an attic too tight, a customer who declined the release), the machine skips that job — scarcity of footage is fine, fabrication is fatal.

Which platform should a shop start with?

Google Business Profile first: the buying intent is already there, the video cap is 30 seconds (easy to fill), and Google's own data ties visuals to 42% more direction requests and 35% more website clicks. TikTok second for reach — one HVAC shop's single tech video pulled 350,000+ plays (TikTok, 2026). Reels and Shorts ride the same exports, so the marginal cost of each additional surface is near zero.
