The constraints: auto-renewal in writing, no dark patterns — and the follow-up needs consent

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because a plan with illegal renewal terms is a lawsuit, not an asset. Gate 1 — auto-renewal law: the FTC's Negative Option Rule and most state automatic-renewal laws (California's is the strictest — others follow its shape) require the renewal terms to be stated clearly before enrollment, separate consent for the auto-renewing feature, and a cancellation path that is at least as simple as enrollment. A pre-checked box, a plan buried in fine print, or a cancellation process that takes three phone calls is the exact pattern these laws target. Every plan enrollment in this playbook carries a one-page plain-English agreement: what's included, what renews when, what it costs at renewal, and how to cancel in one step. Gate 2 — outreach consent: the 48-hour follow-up call/SMS and every renewal reminder are automated outreach. Consent for calls and texts is captured at the job (on the work order the customer signs) and recorded in the CRM before step 1 fires — with a working opt-out on every message. Gate 3 — the AI never enrolls silently: the voice agent presents the plan and answers questions; the customer's yes is recorded with their name and date, and the written agreement goes out immediately after. No plan is ever added to a bill without an explicit spoken yes first. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice — run your agreement past counsel for your state.

The short version

Our missed-call playbook (#1) catches the calls you miss, the quote-chase playbook (#4) recovers the estimates you lose, and the review playbook (#6) harvests the jobs you finish — this playbook turns the jobs you finish into recurring revenue. Every service visit ends with a 60-second plan offer on the tech's tablet, every non-enrolled customer gets an AI follow-up call within 48 hours, and every enrolled member rides an auto-renewal engine with saved billing and a lapsed-member win-back sequence. The demand case is the strongest in this family: contractors with robust membership programs generate 25–40% of total revenue from recurring agreements (ServiceTitan's own published material, compiled July 2026 — a top-operator benchmark; the typical contractor sits closer to 2–5%, which is the opportunity), and 63% of contractors say over half their customer base is secured through a preventative maintenance agreement, with 75% of agreement programs reporting over 20% additional revenue from pull-through work (same compilation). The traditional model's ceiling is low — only 20–25% of homeowners sign up for the classic prepay-and-visit plan, per SmartAC founder Josh Teekell (Contracting Business, September 2026) — but connected memberships moved annual retention from roughly 65–75% to about 95% and lifted average revenue per home 19–20% over two to three years (same source). The money math is consumer-grade: plans run $100–$250 a year (premium tiers up to $500+) while a single tune-up costs $70–250 (USA Today, June 26, 2026) — so the plan pays for itself on two visits before the discounts kick in. The build is Make (the visit-completed trigger, the non-enrolled flag, the renewal sequence), Synthflow (the 48-hour follow-up call), ElevenLabs voices so it sounds like your office, and your field-service software's membership module for the agreement and billing. Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The consent line and the no-dark-pattern line, on paper

Rule 1: no customer enters the follow-up sequence without consent captured at the job and recorded in the CRM. Rule 2: enrollment is always explicit and in writing — the one-page agreement states what's included, the renewal price, the renewal date, and the one-step cancellation path. Print both rules, tape them to the office wall, and train every tech: the plan sells on math, never on confusion. A member who didn't know what they bought is a chargeback, a bad review, and an auto-renewal-law problem — the gate above is the page that keeps this asset an asset.

Step 1 — The visit trigger

1Non-member visit closes → customer flagged

When a service call, repair, or tune-up is marked complete, the field-service software checks membership status. Members get their plan benefits logged automatically. Non-members — the only people this playbook touches — land in the enrollment sequence with three facts attached: what the tech did today, the equipment age and warranty status from the property record, and whether they were offered the plan in the home. Equipment past warranty with a repair today is the highest-value flag: the plan's repair discount makes the offer self-justifying on the spot. This is the same "every visit is a data point" machinery a contractor software vendor used to triple a six-tech shop's agreement revenue in 2026 (Contractor+, February 2026 case study) — we're just aiming it at enrollment instead of dispatch.

Runs on: your field-service software (membership module) + Make for the flag-and-route logic.

Step 2 — The in-home 60-second offer

2The tech, the tablet, and the paper-towel math

The highest-converting enrollment moment is standing in the customer's home, right after the repair is done, with the numbers on a screen. The tech's tablet shows the plan page: two tune-ups a year at list price ($140–$500 combined, USA Today's figures) vs the plan ($100–$250) plus the repair discount and priority scheduling. Sales coach Charlie Greer's paper-towel close — show the customer what the math looks like in writing, let them see the plan saves money rather than costs money — is the oldest version of this conversation in the trade (Contracting Business; Greer's claim of near-total close rates when the tech believes in it is a sales coach's claim, not a measured benchmark). The tablet enrollment is the written yes: plan tier, auto-renewal terms from the gate above, e-signature, card on file. No tablet, no paper form? The tech offers it verbally and the customer enters step 3 — but the in-home close is the target, because every hour that passes cools the yes.

Runs on: field-service software enrollment screen (or a plain tablet form that syncs to the CRM).

Step 3 — The 48-hour AI follow-up

3The ones who didn't enroll get one call

Non-enrolled customers from step 1 get exactly one AI voice call within 48 hours — the system restates the in-home math, answers the two most common questions (what's covered, how cancellation works), and offers to enroll them on the call. The call identifies itself as an automated assistant in the first sentence (gate 3), records an explicit yes with name and date, and the written agreement from the gate above goes out by email/SMS immediately after. Declines are removed from the sequence — one call, no drip harassment, because a pressured enrollment becomes next year's churn statistic. Expectation-setting from field data: one vendor's tune-up automation reports 42% of tune-ups converting to a plan (Fieldproxy, crawled October 2026) — treat that as the high-water mark for a well-run program, not a promise; SmartAC's 20–25% traditional signup ceiling is the floor you're improving on.

Runs on: Synthflow (the call) + ElevenLabs (office-warm voice) + Make (sequence, opt-out honoring).

Step 4 — The auto-renewal engine

4Renewals you don't have to chase

Enrollment is half the system; the renewal engine is the other half. Thirty days before renewal, the member gets a plain-English reminder (what renews, at what price, cancel in one step — the gate above is non-negotiable here). The stored card charges on the renewal date. Members whose cards fail get a two-touch rescue sequence before they lapse. Lapsed members get a win-back at 60 and 120 days with the re-enrollment math — a lapsed member is the cheapest enrollment you'll ever make. The target is the connected-membership retention bar: roughly 95% annual retention vs 65–75% for traditional plans (SmartAC, September 2026). And members are worth more than their dues: agreement programs report 20%+ additional pull-through revenue from repairs and replacements discovered on plan visits (ServiceTitan-published compilation, July 2026) — the plan is a customer-retention system that happens to bill annually.

Runs on: Make (renewal + rescue + win-back sequences) + your membership billing.

Step 5 — The monthly plan-health review

5Count members like leads

First Monday of the month, the owner reads four numbers: members added (in-home vs 48-hour follow-up — which channel closes?), churn (cancellations + failed renewals, against the ~95% retention bar), pull-through revenue per member (repairs and replacements discovered on plan visits), and agreement share of total revenue (the journey from the typical 2–5% toward the 25–40% top-operator band). A concrete reference point for the scale this compounds at: Atlantic Refrigeration grew from 16 agreements to over 50 while service revenue grew from $1M to $1.8M over three years (ServiceTitan-published case study, compiled July 2026). The three-year lens matters more than the monthly one: $2,840 per automated tune-up customer over three years vs $420 without automation (Fieldproxy, crawled October 2026) is the value of the relationship, not the dues.

Build vs buy

The honest read: buy the membership module, build the enrollment motion. Field-service platforms (ServiceTitan's membership tooling, Successware's agreement modules) own agreement tracking, billing, and plan-visit scheduling — that integration load is the real build cost and vendors built it over years. Third-party pricing teardowns put ServiceTitan around $300 per technician per month with implementation fees on top (Projul, 2026 teardown — third-party estimate, not a vendor quote), so the module price is a real budget line. Where the buy stack is weak is exactly this playbook's job: the in-home offer math, the 48-hour follow-up call, and the lapsed-member win-back sequence are motion, not software. A shop already on a field-service platform builds steps 1–4 on Make + a voice agent for a fraction of a new module; a shop with no platform should buy the platform first — automating enrollment on spreadsheets is how plans leak. Independent agreement-first tools (Contractor+'s property-profile auto-renewal, claiming 92% retention in its 2026 case study — vendor claim, dated) sit between: cheaper than a full platform swap, stronger on renewals than a DIY build. Common objections, answered honestly

We already run playbook #4 — isn't this the same system?

Same family, opposite ends of the deal. #4 chases estimates that went quiet — customers who never bought. #8 enrolls customers who already paid for a visit. They share the voice-agent stack and the consent machinery, but they never share a trigger, a script, or a metric — #4 measures estimates recovered, #8 measures plan members and recurring share of revenue. Most contractors should run #4 first (dead quotes are the bigger immediate leak) and add #8 once quotes stop going quiet.

What enrollment rate should we expect?

Set the bar with third-party numbers, not hope. The traditional signup ceiling is 20–25% of homeowners (SmartAC, September 2026). Fieldproxy's tune-up automation reports 42% of tune-ups converting to a plan — treat that as the high-water mark for a well-run program with an in-home offer and a 48-hour follow-up, not a promise. If you're converting 15% of non-member visits in year one, you're inside the honest band; the system exists to walk that number toward the top third over time.

Won't members cost us money — two free tune-ups for $150?

Only if the plan is priced wrong. That's the whole point of the tablet math in step 2: the plan is priced so the two tune-ups cost you less than the dues plus what the customer would have paid à la carte — and members buy 20%+ more pull-through work (repairs, replacements found on plan visits) than non-members. One contractor software vendor's headline advice in its 2026 case study is literally the pricing math so agreements contribute margin instead of subsidizing labor. Price the plan from your true tune-up cost, not from what the shop down the street charges.

Do we need a lawyer for the auto-renewal agreement?

Yes — once, up front, not forever. The one-page agreement needs to satisfy your state's automatic-renewal law (California's shape is the strictest and the common template), and a local business attorney reviews it in one sitting. That's a few hundred dollars against the annual value of a membership base. What the lawyer is checking is exactly gate 1: clear renewal terms before enrollment, separate consent for the auto-renewing feature, and one-step cancellation. After that review, the sequence runs without counsel.

We're a two-truck shop — is this overkill?

Yes, as a system — and the page says so. Two trucks don't need a 48-hour AI follow-up; the tech makes the offer in the home and the owner calls the maybe-laters himself on Friday. What a small shop should take from this playbook is the one-page agreement (the legal gate is size-independent) and the monthly member count. The automation earns its cost when the visit volume makes the follow-up inconsistent — roughly when non-member visits exceed what one person can call back in a week. Build the rules now, automate when the volume demands it.
