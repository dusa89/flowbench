The consent gates: new movers are the most regulated audience you'll ever market to

This playbook runs on contact — and contact is where the family finally has to print the compliance box before the verdict, because every touch in it sits on a federal rule. Gate 1: no autodialed or pre-recorded calls or texts to a cell phone without prior express written consent (federal TCPA — liability up to $1,500 per violation). You do not buy a scraped cell-phone list and text the movers. The Step-3 welcome call is placed manually or is opt-in; the AI voice agent only calls numbers that gave consent at booking or on the packet's reply card. Gate 2: B2C cold email marketing needs an opt-out, truthful headers, and your physical address on every message (CAN-SPAM) — and even when it's compliant, cold email is the weakest leg of this machine; the mail and the call carry it. Gate 3: USPS Every Door Direct Mail (EDDM) is your compliant saturation channel — it hits every address on a mail route without a list at all, so the list rules never fire. Gate 4: no pre-checked consent boxes, ever — the packet's reply card and the binder's service-request form collect consent the real way, unchecked box, plain language, one tap to withdraw. Gate 5: honor every opt-out the same day and keep a do-not-contact list with dates, forever. This page is the honest first-vendor machine: mail you can send, calls you may make, records you may hold — nothing that buys you a lawsuit before the first job.

The short version

Every playbook in this family so far rescues a pipeline the shop already has — the missed call, the dead quote, the dormant customer. None of them race to be the first vendor a new household picks, and the math says that's the best race in the family. National Association of Home Builders research reports that buyers of newly built single-family homes spend an average of $26,882 on appliances, furnishings, remodeling and repairs during their first year — nearly three times what a non-moving homeowner spends (via Dataman Group Direct, EIN Presswire, September 23, 2026). Industry tracking puts new-mover transition spending near $180B a year, with a new homeowner spending more in the first six months after moving than in the previous two years combined (Porch Group Media 2026 data, via mcgrawnow) — and Zillow's survey data puts the average mover's move-related spend near $10,000 (via Deluxe Corporation's new-mover white paper). The loyalty math is the weapon: new movers are five times more likely to become long-term customers if you reach them first than an established household is (Deluxe, 2026), and 90% are open to trying new brands (MarketingCharts) — 30 to 35 million Americans move in a typical year (mcgrawnow, 2026), and roughly 80% of new movers respond to local offers when settling in (Our Town America new-mover guide, 2025). But the window has a decay curve: a new-homeowner record is a freshness problem, not a volume problem — a 10-day-old record is worth far more than a 90-day one, and by 120 days the household has locked in its vendors (infofree's 2026 new-homeowner marketing analysis, crawled October 2026). So the play is a 5-step machine: build the address-change record feed, put the 72-hour welcome packet in the mailbox, make the 7-day manual welcome call, run the 30-day binder check-in, and work the neighbor ladder. The shop that is first in the mailbox on a 10-day-old record becomes the household's plumber, electrician, and HVAC company for a decade. Scored 8.6/10.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you mail anything

0Rule one: first in the mailbox wins — but only if the record is fresh. Rule two: the move-in is the job, not the mailer.

Tape both to the monitor. Rule one: this whole playbook stands or falls on the record feed. Infofree's 2026 analysis is explicit: a 10-day-old new-homeowner record is worth significantly more than a 90-day-old one, and a 120-day-old record is a household that has already picked its vendors — every automation you build in this playbook (Steps 1–5) serves record freshness first, then volume. A 500-record list that's 100 days old loses to a 50-record list that's 10 days old, every time. Rule two: do not sell a coupon. A new mover facing 10–15 simultaneous service decisions (infofree, 2026) doesn't need 10% off — they need one trustworthy name for each trade. The packet in Step 2 is a vendor relationship disguised as a welcome gift, and every touch after it earns the next decade's jobs: the furnace that dies in year three, the panel that can't take the EV charger, the water heater that gives up at year nine. The move-in job pays once; the relationship pays twelve times.

Step 1 — The record feed: 10-day-old addresses, nothing older than 30

1County deed filings + USPS change-of-address targeting + Meta new-mover behaviors, refreshed weekly

Between 30 and 35 million Americans move in a typical year (mcgrawnow, 2026), and Upswell's new-mover tracking puts the share at 9.8% of Americans with average 12-month spend near $8,068 — your service zone has hundreds of these households a month. Build the feed from three sources: (1) county deed filings — new-owner deeds are public record in most counties, often scrapeable or purchasable through the recorder's office; owner-occupied single-family is your filter (skip investors and rentals); (2) USPS change-of-address targeting — licensed NCOA/change-of-address data vendors sell new-mover address files by zip, the cleanest trigger for the packet; (3) Meta advertising's new-mover behaviors — platform-reported "recently moved" segments for a paid reminder layer, not the lead channel. Rules from Step 0: refresh weekly, cap every record at 30 days old, and work the 10-day bucket first — the infofree decay curve (2026) says 120-day records are dead money. The sheet holds name, address, move date, record age in days, owner-vs-renter flag, home age if the deed data carries it, touch log, and opt-out flag. Zero-build version: one sheet, one weekly deed pull, USPS EDDM for the packet (no list needed — Gate 3) — you can run this with zero automation on under 100 records a month. The Make scenario comes in Step 2.

Tools leg: Google Sheets + USPS EDDM (usps.com, public); trigger-mail vendor lists for the DIY upgrade — their freshness and delivery claims are their own, unverified here. [Make affiliate placeholder]

Step 2 — The 72-hour welcome packet: the mail, the binder, the free check

2One oversized welcome mailer within 72 hours of the record — 85% more responsive than ads

New movers are up to 85% more responsive to direct mail than established households (fotorecord's new-mover statistics, crawled October 2026), and half of a new mover's discretionary spending happens in the first six months (Our Town America, 2025) — the mail is not a coupon drop, it's a deadline. The Make scenario fires within 72 hours of a record landing: an oversized postcard or letter to the new address (EDDM route saturation or addressed trigger mail — first-class trigger mailers can hit the mail stream within 24 hours of a data feed, per trigger-mail vendor claims; third-party claim, see build-vs-buy). Inside: the welcome letter from the owner by name (never "dear homeowner"), the home binder — a physical folder with equipment-age log sheets (furnace, water heater, panel, roof), the local trades directory page you curate, and the reply card with the unchecked consent box (Gate 4) — plus the wedge: a free 4-point new-home health check (safety-first visual inspection of panel, water heater, furnace flue, and the main shutoff location walk). The wedge is free because the check is how you find the $967 windows job, the $959 roof conversation, and the $993 security decision (Porch Group Media 2026 category spends, via mcgrawnow) — it is the family's audit-wedge logic applied to a move-in. Track: packet sent date, scan-back code per record, check bookings per 100 packets — the family's whole math rests on packets per week, not dollars per packet.

Tools leg: Make (record → 72-hour packet trigger, scan-back logging) + print-and-mail vendor or USPS EDDM (DIY). [Make affiliate placeholder]

Step 3 — The 7-day call: the welcome, not the sale

3A manual welcome call on day 7 — never autodialed, never a robotext

Day 7 after the packet lands, a human makes a manual call — manual dialing keeps you outside the TCPA autodialer rules (Gate 1), which is the entire reason this call is human-first. The script is 90 seconds: welcome to the neighborhood, the binder has the equipment log (ask whether they've found the main water shutoff — most haven't, and that's the service hook), the free health check is good for 30 days, and the one question that does the real work: "what's the oldest piece of equipment in the house?" That answer becomes the end-of-life ledger entry you act on for a decade. Every call ends with explicit consent for the Step-4 check-in and the packet's mail cadence — and the consent is logged with date and channel, per the gates. The AI voice agent (Synthflow/ElevenLabs) can run this call after consent exists — never as the first touch, and it self-identifies as the shop's assistant in the first five seconds. Call outcomes route three ways: booked check (schedule now), consent given no booking (30-day check-in list), declined (opt-out list, mail stops, Gates 4–5). Two-truck reality: the owner makes these calls for 20 minutes after dinner; at 100 packets a month that's the whole job.

Tools leg: manual dialer list from the sheet; Synthflow + ElevenLabs voice agent for the post-consent variant (verified Oct 1–2). [Synthflow affiliate placeholder] [ElevenLabs affiliate placeholder]

Step 4 — The 30-day binder check-in: where the ledger becomes revenue

4One AI or human check-in at 30 days: equipment ages, warranties, the first utility bill

At 30 days post-move the household is settled enough to have questions and early enough that the vendors aren't locked in — this is the last high-leverage touch before the decay curve wins. The check-in (AI voice agent with consent on file, or the owner's call) runs the binder script: confirm the equipment ages from Step 3 (furnace year, water-heater year, panel amperage), walk the warranty-registration deadlines (most mover-relevant warranties must be registered within 30–90 days of install — flag the ones the seller's disclosure mentioned), and ask about the first utility bill (a spike means the HVAC deserves the health check they haven't booked). Three routes: book the free check (Step 2's wedge) → enroll in the maintenance plan (playbook #8's whole machine — the plan-enrollment page is the natural sequel, and the mover's equipment log is the enrollment form pre-filled) → capture the review after any completed work (playbook #6 — the review stockpile starts on day 30, not year 2). Nothing else: no coupon book, no drip campaign, no 12-touch sequence — the infofree decay curve says the window is closing, so this touch earns the second job, and the second job is what locks in the 5x-long-term-customer math (Deluxe, 2026).

Tools leg: the consent flag from Step 3, the sheet, the AI voice agent legs. [Synthflow affiliate placeholder]

Step 5 — The neighbor ladder: the mover becomes the referrer

5A new mover builds 72 new business relationships — make yours the one they recommend

The average new mover develops 72 new business relationships after a move (fotorecord, crawled October 2026) — which means every mover you win is a node, not a customer. After the first completed job: the one-tap referral ask (playbook #14's engine — the satisfied-signal trigger fires on the completed new-mover job, and the mover's neighbors are also buying 72 relationships right now), the equipment end-of-life ledger entry (furnace at 14 years, water heater at 11 — the shop that logged the age at move-in calls at year 12 with the replacement number ready, while competitors quote blind), and the 12-month move-in anniversary card (one mailer, the health-check renewal, the plan reminder — this is the only drip in the whole machine). The math compounds: each mover who becomes a referral source shortens the next mover's trust window, and the 5x long-term-customer figure (Deluxe, 2026) is what turns one packet into a decade of trade jobs. What this page does NOT do: chase renters — the binder logic needs an owner who controls the equipment, so renter-heavy records get the welcome letter only, and the budget goes to owner-occupied.

The re-skin check — why this is its own playbook

#6 harvests reputation from the jobs you finish; #8 enrolls the customers you already have; #15 chases damage footprints; #16 wins someone else's portfolio. #17 wins the household before anyone else does. Five different jobs. #6's trigger is a completed job and its output is review stock; #17's trigger is an address change and its output is a first-vendor relationship — the move-in happens before any job exists, and #6's review capture is one of #17's downstream steps, not its twin. #8's buyer is an existing customer paying for their own furnace; #17's buyer hasn't picked a vendor for anything — the plan-enrollment machine is the sequel, fed by the equipment log this page builds. #15's trigger is a weather event and its demand is a damage footprint of non-customers; #17's trigger is a deed filing and its demand is $26,882 of first-year spend (NAHB, via Dataman/EINPresswire, September 2026) concentrated in one household. #16's buyer is a portfolio operator buying reliability at scale; #17's is the homeowner buying their first plumber. And it passes the killed pool: warranty-expiration was killed as subsumed, but this page never sends a reminder about expiring equipment — it logs equipment ages at move-in to build the end-of-life ledger, which is the opposite mechanic. Seasonal-reminder and schedule-gap were killed as calendar plays; this page has no calendar marketing and no booking-calendar mechanics — its clock is the record-age decay curve. The hooks are clean: #2's wedge is the free health check this page offers; #8's plan is the 30-day destination; #14's referral engine is the neighbor ladder's ask. This page ends at the 12-month anniversary card. A page that tried to harvest reviews or sell maintenance plans inside it would swallow two siblings.

Frequently asked questions

I'm a two-truck shop. Can I actually run a weekly record feed?

The honest read: run the zero-build version first and it is a Friday-afternoon job. The county deed pull is one spreadsheet a week, the EDDM route saturation covers the new-address routes without any list, and the Step-3 calls are 20 minutes after dinner at 100 packets a month. The Make scenario (Step 1–2) pays for itself the month you cross ~150 new-owner records a month — below that, the sheet and the post office are faster than the automation. What the two-truck shop must not skip is the record-age cap: stale records are how small shops burn postage on households that picked a vendor 90 days ago (infofree, 2026). Thirty days maximum, ten days first — the cap is the strategy.

Why not just text the new movers? Everyone texts.

Because that is the TCPA trap (Gate 1). Buying a scraped cell list and texting new homeowners is autodialed marketing text to numbers without prior express written consent — the federal liability is up to $1,500 per violation, and a 1,000-text campaign is a bankruptcy event, not a marketing test. This playbook's entire contact design exists to avoid that: compliant mail (EDDM/trigger mail), a manual day-7 call, AI voice only after consent is on file, and the consent captured on the reply card and the binder form. The 85% direct-mail responsiveness figure (fotorecord) is the honest consolation — the channel you're allowed to use is also the channel this audience answers best.

Isn't this just direct mail with a fancy name?

The mail is one touch in a four-touch machine with a clock. Direct mail without the record feed is what the build-vs-buy table calls "going it alone" — random welcome mail with no freshness is the spend, not the save, because the infofree decay curve (2026) says a 120-day record is a household that's already chosen. What makes this a playbook instead of a mailer: the 10/90/120 freshness rules, the 72-hour packet trigger, the day-7 manual call with the equipment-age question, the 30-day check-in that routes to plan enrollment (#8) and review capture (#6), and the neighbor ladder that turns one mover into referrals to the 72 relationships (fotorecord) every new mover is building. The mailer is the door; the machine is the house.

Should I target renters or only buyers?

Buyers first, always — the whole machine is built on the $26,882 first-year spend figure (NAHB, via Dataman/EINPresswire, September 2026) and on equipment the mover controls. A renter doesn't buy a furnace; they call the landlord. Step 5 says it plainly: renter-heavy records get the welcome letter only, and the packet/binder budget goes to owner-occupied. The exception is build-to-rent communities and single-family rentals — those tenants are a property manager's problem, and playbook #16 is the page that wins them. Don't split the budget: one playbook, one buyer.

How long until the first job from this machine?

Honest: 2–6 weeks from first packet to first booked health check, and the real money is months 2–12. The packet and the day-7 call win the wedge — the free 4-point check — and the check finds the repair and replacement work. The 5x long-term-customer figure (Deluxe, 2026) is a retention number, not a same-week number: this is the slowest first-job playbook in the consumer family, and the page says so because the payoff is the decade. Budget it like #16's 60–90-day honesty, with one difference: the volume never stops — 30–35 million Americans move every year (mcgrawnow, 2026), so the feed refills itself every month, forever. What you measure at 90 days is packets per week, record-age distribution, and check bookings per 100 packets — not revenue yet. The revenue is the ledger at year 3.
