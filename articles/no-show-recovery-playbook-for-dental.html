The constraints: HIPAA is a gate, not a checkbox — and the short-notice list gets consent at intake

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because a dental schedule is protected health information. Gate 1 — HIPAA: appointment data is PHI. Only HIPAA-capable tooling with a signed business associate agreement (BAA) touches the schedule or patient list — the same box as our dental voice-agent playbook (#2). In practice that means the reminder/consent layer runs on a HIPAA-flagged tool (the #2 playbook documents the Trillet $49/month non-PHI lane versus Voicify's quote-gated HIPAA fork as the fork point) and nothing carrying patient identity or visit detail ever flows through a non-BAA automation or model. Gate 2 — outreach consent: the AI fill call and the short-notice texts are automated outreach, governed by the TCPA in the US and CASL in Canada. Consent is captured at intake or first booking ("we may text or call about your appointments and openings that fit you") and recorded in the practice management system before step 1 fires — and every automated message carries a working opt-out. This is the same discipline the established vendors observe: Solutionreach, for example, notes TCPA and CASL support and asks staff to check each message against them before sending (softwarefinder.com, vendor page updated September 2, 2026). Gate 3 — the AI identifies itself: the fill call opens by saying it is the practice's automated scheduler — never a front-desk staffer — and hands to a human the moment a patient sounds confused, upset, or in pain. A patient in pain is triaged to the on-call line like our after-hours playbook (#5), never left in automation. And the playbook's founding rule: a no-show is a schedule event, never a blame event. The patient who didn't show gets rebooked with dignity, not lectured by a machine. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice.

The short version

Our missed-call playbooks (#1 for contractors, #2 for dental) catch the calls you miss — this playbook catches the appointments that go empty. The trigger is the schedule itself, not the phone: a no-show flag or a late cancellation opens a chair slot, and a ranked short-notice waitlist starts working it in minutes, because the refill window is minutes, not hours — ten minutes is the target, not the same afternoon (hamzaasumah.org, September 2026). The AI calls down the waitlist in order and books the first patient who says yes straight into the practice management schedule; the patient who didn't show gets a separate, dignity-first rebook path. Reminders already do the prevention work — automated reminders cut dental no-shows 25–45% (compilation via hamzaasumah.org, September 2026) — but the residual is still the industry's biggest recoverable leak: Planet DDS's 2025 data across 3,400 practices puts the no-show rate at 7.4% with 15.5% in advance cancellations (via iSedate's 2026 compilation, crawled October 2026); without automated reminders the American Dental Association's benchmark is 30%, and new-patient no-shows run 35–40% (2026 industry compilation via trtc.io, crawled October 2026). At $200–$400 in lost production per missed appointment and $500–$800 per empty chair hour, the annual cost lands at $105,000–$300,000+ per provider (iSedate 2026, crawled October 2, 2026). And the recovery math is measured, not hopeful: short-notice waitlists are accepted 25–40% of the time, a 10-point no-show reduction is worth $60,000–$120,000 recovered against $4,000–$10,000 in technology cost, and one study of 1,604,184 appointments across 64 practices cut no-shows 22.95% (compilation via hamzaasumah.org, September 2026). The build is Make (the schedule watch, the ranked waitlist engine, the SMS branch), Synthflow (the AI fill call — consent-gated, self-identifying), and ElevenLabs voices so the call sounds like your office. All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The consent line and the no-blame line, on paper

Rule one: the short-notice list is opt-in at intake. Every new patient is asked, plainly: "If a slot opens sooner than yours, may we text or call to offer it?" The answer is recorded as a structured field (consent date, channel) in the practice management system — this is gate 2 of the hard box, and the waitlist is dead without it. Rule two: no-shows are handled by policy, not by robot. Write the practice's missed-appointment policy on one page — rebook-first language, how the team tightens confirmation after a second miss, what the cancellation window is — and sign it off with counsel. The automation in this playbook never invents policy, never lectures, and never penalizes; it recovers the slot and rebooks the patient. Same family principle as #2's HIPAA box: the system enforces the written rule, it doesn't write the rule.

Built with: the practice's existing consent field in the PMS — no new software; Make holds a copy of the ranked list (see step 2).

Step 1 — The empty-slot trigger

1Schedule watch: no-show flag, late-cancel flag, gap opened

Make watches the schedule three ways: a no-show flag from the front desk (the appointment starts and the patient isn't there), a late-cancellation event (patient cancels inside the practice's window), and a gap detection (a slot sits unfilled within 72 hours). Each trigger fires with the same payload: procedure type and chair time needed, provider, date/time of the slot, and how long until the slot expires. The payload carries no PHI outside HIPAA-gated systems — gate 1 runs through every step. The trigger also opens the timer: the practice's rule is that every empty slot gets a first fill attempt within 10 minutes of the flag, per the hamzaasumah ten-minute rule above. The rest of the playbook exists to make that SLA automatic instead of heroic.

Built with: Make — the schedule watcher (PMS webhook, email-to-webhook bridge, or SMS-flag relay for systems without an API; integration load is disclosed — see FAQ), the 10-minute SLA timer.

Step 2 — The ranked short-notice waitlist

2Built at booking, ordered by fit, not first-come

The waitlist is the engine. It is built every time a patient books or confirms: "Want first dibs if something opens sooner?" Opt-ins land on a ranked list — not first-come-first-served, but ordered by procedure fit (does this patient need the chair time this slot offers?), then by how long they've waited, then by provider preference and distance. When step 1 fires, Make scores the list against the open slot and queues the top three to five candidates. The list is a living thing: patients roll off when booked, pause when they decline twice in a month, and re-confirm their spot at every recall visit. This is the difference from a reminder system: reminders shout at the patient who might not come; the waitlist talks to the patients who want to come sooner. Published waitlist fill benchmarks exist in the buy market — Luma Health's "Smart Waitlist" product ships with published fill-rate benchmarks (via emitrr.com, crawled October 2026) — which tells you this lane is proven enough that vendors name numbers.

Built with: Make — the ranked-list data store (patients keyed by procedure type, wait duration, preference, consent state); refreshes on every booking/confirmation.

Step 3 — The AI fill call

3Call down the list in order, book the first yes

Synthflow calls the ranked candidates in order, each call self-identifying in the first sentence: "This is the automated scheduler for [practice] — a slot opened today at [time] for [procedure]. Would you like it?" A yes books straight into the PMS and stops the cascade; a no or no-answer moves to the next candidate; a third strike triggers the SMS branch — a two-way text with a one-tap "CLAIM" link and the front-desk number. The human fallback is explicit: if nobody claims the slot within the 10-minute window, the system pages the front desk with the slot details and the three closest candidates, so the recovery attempt is never worse than what the practice did before. The voice is an ElevenLabs office-warm voice — the family rule — never a chipper robot, never pretending to be staff. Two measured anchors for this step: waitlist offers are accepted 25–40% of the time (compilation via hamzaasumah.org, September 2026), and 20–35% of the no-show revenue loss is recoverable (same compilation) — which is why the cascade keeps calling: a few attempts at 30% acceptance is how a 60%+ slot-fill rate happens.

Built with: Synthflow (the fill call, self-identifying script, SMS branch) + ElevenLabs (the voice — a warm office voice, never a chipper robot) + Make (the cascade engine, the 10-minute window, the front-desk fallback page).

Step 4 — The no-show patient's own recovery

4Rebook with dignity, tighten the confirmation

This is a separate path from the fill cascade — and the reason gate 3 exists. The patient who didn't show gets their own call, on their own cadence: a Synthflow rebook call that opens with understanding, not policy — "We missed you today. Are you all right? Can we find you a new time?" — and offers two concrete slots. A second consecutive miss triggers the practice's written confirmation tightening (card-hold language is the practice's own written policy, never invented by the automation). Note what this step is not: it is not a punishment, not a lecture, and not a review-style blast. No-show patients are future revenue — a new patient who vanishes after booking is already-paid-for marketing spend walking away (one 2026 practice audit of new-patient show rates put the monthly leak near six figures, documented in a Root Data walkthrough video, 2026). Recovering them is worth more than replacing them.

Built with: Synthflow (the rebook call — dignity-first script, two-slot offer) + Make (the consecutive-miss counter, the policy-escalation branch).

Step 5 — The monthly schedule-leakage check

5Count recovered slots like leads

Once a month, the practice pulls four numbers: no-show rate and late-cancel rate (against the Planet DDS 7.4% / 15.5% anchors), slot-fill rate (empty slots that got booked over empty slots that opened), recovered production dollars (filled slots × average appointment value), and waitlist health (opt-ins per 100 bookings, decline-pauses). The recovery benchmark to beat: $60,000–$120,000 recovered per 10-point no-show reduction against $4,000–$10,000 in technology cost (compilation via hamzaasumah.org, September 2026). If the fill rate climbs but the no-show rate doesn't fall, the win is the waitlist — the confirmation tightening in step 4 needs the practice's attention. Same as every playbook in the family: the numbers decide what gets tuned next.

Built with: Make — the monthly rollup (slot events → the four metrics) + a one-page report the owner reads in five minutes.

How this fits the family

That's the re-skin check, stated plainly: #2 (our dental voice-agent playbook) recovers the new patient who hung up without booking; #7 recovers the booked patient who never sat in the chair and fills the chair they left behind. #4 (estimate recovery) is the closest home-services cousin — both work a list over time — but #4 chases the quote that never closed while #7 fills the slot that just opened, on a 10-minute clock. #5 (after-hours dispatch triage) is the sibling on the other side: #5 keeps live emergencies from reaching voicemail after hours; #7 keeps empty chairs from staying empty during hours. Common objections, answered honestly

We already run playbook #2 — isn't this the same system?

No — same AI, different event. #2 answers a question about the phone: "who tried to reach us and didn't book?" #7 answers a question about the schedule: "which slots went empty, and who's on the short-notice list to take them?" The consent gate, the HIPAA box, and the voice stack are shared — the triggers, the lists, and the scripts are entirely separate. A practice can run both without double-calling anyone, because the two never call the same patient about the same thing.

Aren't SMS reminders enough?

Reminders are the prevention layer, and they work — automated confirmation cuts no-shows 25–45% (compilation via hamzaasumah.org, September 2026). But even with reminders, the ADA's benchmark is 14–18% no-shows, plus the 15.5% advance cancellations the Planet DDS data records. Reminders shrink the leak; they don't fill the slot it leaves behind. This playbook is the recovery layer on top of reminders — prevention first, recovery second, always both.

What about HIPAA with AI calling patients?

Same box as our dental voice-agent playbook (#2): appointment data is PHI, so only HIPAA-capable tooling with a signed BAA touches the schedule or patient list. The AI never receives more than it needs — the fill call carries the slot, the procedure type, and the patient's opt-in; clinical detail never leaves the PMS. Review this with counsel before you automate, the same as any patient outreach.

Won't patients find an AI call creepy?

They find it creepy when it pretends to be human. The script self-identifies in the first sentence as the practice's automated scheduler — the same honesty rule as every playbook in the family — and the call exists because the patient opted into the short-notice list. Nobody is surprised by a call they asked for. The human fallback is explicit: confusion, upset, or pain hands to a person immediately.

What if our practice management software has no API?

That's the heaviest integration step, and we're honest about it: it's where most DIY builds stall. Three routes exist. One, buy the integration: Solutionreach's 400+ EHR/PM integrations are the moat in the build-vs-buy table above. Two, bridge it: Make can watch for schedule events through email-to-webhook bridges, SMS-flag relays from the front desk, or a scheduled export poll — slower than a native webhook, but enough for the 10-minute SLA in most shops. Three, the hybrid: run the fill cascade on the build and keep confirmation inside the PMS's own tools. Pick the route that fits the PMS, not the philosophy.
