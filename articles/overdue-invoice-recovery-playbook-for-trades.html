The constraints: you are the creditor, not a collector — and the law cares about tone

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because a reminder that breaks collection rules is a liability, not a cash-flow tool. Gate 1 — say only true things: this playbook is first-party outreach — you chasing your own invoice — which is how most small shops operate. Never imply a lawsuit, a lien filing, or a credit report that you do not intend to file. A threat you don't carry out is the fastest way to lose the customer and, in some states, to break the law. Gate 2 — late fees live in the agreement, not in the reminder: any late fee or interest you apply must be disclosed in your payment terms before the job starts, and the rate must comply with your state's rules (contractors' late-fee guides note that low usury-cap states warrant a conversation with a local attorney before a rate is set). Gate 3 — consent and identity: reminder texts and calls need consent captured at the job (on the signed work order) with a working opt-out on every message, and any AI voice agent identifies itself in the first sentence as calling on behalf of your company — it never impersonates a human, a lawyer, or a collections agency. For trades, mechanics-lien rights are the real leverage behind a final notice — but lien rules, deadlines, and preliminary-notice requirements are state-specific, so the lien step goes through your attorney, not an automation. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice — run your terms and your lien process past counsel for your state.

The short version

Our quote-chase playbook (#4) recovers the estimates you lost before the sale — this playbook collects the invoices customers owe after the work is done. It's a different job with a different trigger: #4 starts with silence and a dollar amount on a quote; this starts with a signed invoice for work already delivered, now sitting past its terms. The sequence is the AR aging ladder: invoice crosses terms → gentle reminders (Day 1–14) → a firm-but-fair AI collection call (Day 21–30) → final written notice with the disclosed late fee (Day 45) → owner decision on lien or collections. The demand case is brutally simple: a 2026 QuickBooks survey found 59% of US small businesses had invoices more than 30 days overdue, and businesses with unpaid invoices were owed $17,700 on average (via Joist's plumbing-contractor invoicing guide, crawled October 2026). Sage's June 2026 SME Performance Pulse: 49% of small-business invoices were overdue, with payment arriving an average 27 days beyond agreed terms. The Federation of Small Businesses estimates ~50,000 UK businesses close each year from late-payment cash-flow problems (reported by Business Money, September 2026). In construction specifically, ChaserHQ's 2026 AR statistics show the sector has the worst payment timing of any industry, with 55% of construction businesses waiting 31 or more days past the due date. And the fix is embarrassingly mechanical: a $4M civil subcontractor sat on $287,000 in receivables averaging 52 days outstanding — "the GC is slow because no one is pushing; a systematic collection call on Monday morning changes that within two weeks" (constructioncfo.net, August 2026). One AR consultant reports $2.1M+ in client AR recovered since 2023 with process, not loans (same source). For solo shops the stakes are personal: solo practitioners lose an estimated ~$10,000+/year to unpaid invoices because, unlike large firms, they have no dedicated AR staff — overdue accounts just sit (Medium, March 2026). The build is Make (the aging-bucket triggers, the reminder ladder, the QuickBooks/CRM sync), Synthflow (the firm Day-21–30 collection call), ElevenLabs voices so the call sounds like your office, and your accounting software's AR aging report as the source of truth. All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The tone rule and the paper rule, on the office wall

Rule 1 — reminders are service, never threats. Every message in this sequence could be read aloud in front of a judge without embarrassing you: polite, factual, and true. The sequence collects faster because it is consistent, not because it is scary — most late payers simply forgot, and the shop that asks first gets paid first. Rule 2 — the invoice goes out the same day, with real terms. Joist's contractor invoicing guide (October 2026) puts same-day invoicing as the single biggest lever: send it before you leave the property, with an actual term on it — due on receipt, Net 7, or Net 15 — not "net 30" by default when the customer assumed pay-on-completion. Late fees and terms go in the signed work order, not in a surprise on the reminder. A customer can't pay a term they never agreed to. Both rules get printed and taped to the office wall — the sequence below never fires without them.

Step 1 — The aging trigger

1Invoice crosses terms → lands in its aging bucket

The trigger is the AR aging report, not a calendar date someone remembers. Invoices bucket into current / 30 / 60 / 90+ days, and the bucket decides the message. A healthy AR book has most balances in current and 30 — a growing 90+ bucket is the collection process failing early, when it was still cheap to fix (constructioncfo.net, August 2026). Pair the buckets with DSO (days sales outstanding): DSO gives you the direction of travel, the buckets tell you which invoices are driving it — read both together every month. Make watches the accounting system for an invoice crossing its due date and opens the sequence with the invoice's facts attached: amount, age, whether a late fee was disclosed, and whether the customer is a repeat slow-payer. This is the "nobody forgets to ask" layer — the shop no longer discovers a 90-day invoice by accident.

Runs on: your accounting software's AR aging report (QuickBooks/Xero/FreshBooks-style aging) + Make for the bucket-and-route logic.

Step 2 — The gentle ladder (Day 1–14)

2Three reminders, each a little less ignorable

Day 1 past terms: a friendly email + SMS — "Invoice #___ for $___ was due [date]; attaching a copy and a pay link." Day 7: a second notice, still friendly — "Checking in — did the invoice land OK?" Day 14: the "anything disputed?" message — this one exists to surface the hidden problem: work-quality disputes and billing confusion hide inside polite silence, and finding them at Day 14 is weeks cheaper than at Day 60. Any reply, any dispute flag, any payment promise routes to the owner immediately — the sequence stops and a human takes over. The tone stays service-framed the whole way: the shop that helps you pay is not the shop you're annoyed at when the bill arrives.

Runs on: Make (the Day-1/7/14 sequence, reply/dispute routing, pay-link generation) + your accounting software.

Step 3 — The firm call (Day 21–30)

3An AI voice call that is firm, kind, and specific

Day 21–30: the AI voice agent calls — firm but warm, identifying itself in the first sentence as calling on behalf of your company. The script does four things: (1) names the exact invoice, amount, and due date; (2) asks for payment on the call via the pay link; (3) offers the two legitimate off-ramps — a split payment with dates, or a dispute review with the owner; (4) records whatever the customer says verbatim in the CRM. No threats, no invented deadlines, no legal language — just presence. This call exists because a surprising share of late payment is avoidance, and a real voice at Day 25 moves more accounts than six more emails. The agent is trained on the tone rule from step 0: it never impersonates, never bluffs, and hands any heat to the owner within the same call.

Runs on: Synthflow (the Day-21–30 collection call) + ElevenLabs (voice quality for call realism) + Make (scheduling off the aging bucket, logging outcomes to the CRM).

Step 4 — The final notice and the owner decision (Day 45)

4Final written notice → owner picks the escalation

Day 45: a final written notice goes out — the invoice, the disclosed late fee applied per your terms, and a hard pay-by date. This is where the automation stops pretending and the owner decides. The decision tree has three branches: (a) keep collecting personally for accounts worth the relationship; (b) file the mechanics lien for trades where the work and deadlines qualify — through your attorney, never through an automation, because preliminary-notice deadlines vary by state and a blown deadline is the lien's most common failure mode; (c) hand the account to a collections service (Medium, March 2026: handing off an account takes about 10 minutes — the friction was never the handoff, it was knowing when). Repeat slow-payers get flagged for the prevention loop in step 5: the shop that fires bad payers politely is the shop whose AR stays clean.

Runs on: Make (the Day-45 notice, the flag to the owner with the account's full history) + your attorney for any lien step.

Step 5 — The prevention loop

5Make slow payers extinct instead of just managed

The sequence collects cash; the loop fixes the faucet. Same-day invoicing becomes non-negotiable (Joist's guide again: before you leave the property). Jobs above your threshold take a deposit. Repeat customers move to a saved card on file. And once a quarter, the owner reviews the slow-payer list — the customers who paid late twice are told politely that future work needs payment on completion. This is the step vendors never sell because it doesn't need software: the contractor who only works for people who pay on time doesn't have an AR problem. Run the ladder for everyone else; run the loop so next year's AR book is boring.

Runs on: your accounting software (payment terms, deposits, saved billing) + a quarterly owner review — no automation required.

Common questions

Won't reminder messages anger good customers?

The opposite, usually. The Day-1 message is framed as service — "did the invoice land OK?" — and most late payers simply forgot; the shop that asks first gets paid first. The tone rule from step 0 is the whole guardrail: nothing in this sequence would embarrass you read aloud. What angers customers is being surprised at Day 90, not reminded at Day 7.

What if the customer disputes the work?

Then the sequence did its job. The Day-14 "anything disputed?" message is a dispute-detector — finding the dispute at Day 14 is weeks cheaper than discovering it when the account lands at Day 90. Any dispute stops the automation and routes to the owner immediately; a disputed invoice never gets a firm call.

Is an AI collection call legal?

This page is not legal advice — but the design follows the conservative pattern: the agent identifies itself as calling on behalf of your company in the first sentence, makes no threats it doesn't intend to carry out, and consent for the outreach was captured on the signed work order with a working opt-out. First-party outreach (you chasing your own invoice) operates under different rules than third-party debt collectors. Run your script and terms past counsel for your state before going live.

Should I charge late fees?

Contractors' late-fee guides make the honest point: a late fee's real job isn't the money it collects — it's making on-time payment the easier, cheaper option for the client. At a typical 1.5%/month, a $5,000 invoice accrues $150 over two months — small enough that most clients would rather just pay on time (Joist late-fee guide, crawled October 2026). The catch: the fee must be in your terms before the job, and the rate must comply with your state's rules — talk to a local attorney in low-usury-cap states before setting one.

I only do residential work. Do I need the full 45-day ladder?

Residential invoices are usually smaller and simpler — but the ladder still applies, just compressed. Most residential late payment resolves at Day 1 or Day 7 (forgotten invoice, lost email). The two-truck shop's honest read: steps 1–2 plus the prevention loop recover the bulk of the money; steps 3–4 exist for the stubborn 5–10% and for commercial/property-management customers on longer terms. Don't skip step 5 either way — deposits and saved cards on file are what make residential AR boring.
