The discipline gates: software doesn't stock a van — five gates, non-negotiable

Every vendor selling van-stock software will show you the same magic numbers. Read the gates before you quote them. Gate 1: the scan discipline IS the system. A barcode app no tech opens is a shelf of sticky labels. The playbook's whole Step 1 is behavior, not software — the shop that wins here wins on a 90-second end-of-day scan habit, not on the platform it bought. Gate 2: the vendor numbers are vendor numbers. The plumbing blueprints below come from Fieldproxy, a vendor selling field-service automation (crawled October 2026, labeled throughout) — 3–5 hours per tech per week lost, 85% fewer emergency trips, 95–98% stock accuracy. Treat those as the ceiling of what's achievable, not the floor of what's typical. The third-party numbers — the $220 run, the 5–10% profit erasure, the Aberdeen halved-stockout finding — are the floor. Gate 3: shrinkage accounting has an honesty requirement. When you find a stockout, the first move is the write-down, not the witch hunt: most missing parts are unrecorded transfers, double issues to one job, or bins nobody labeled — not theft. Investigate after you record, and keep people notes out of the system until you have a pattern (Supply Chain Digest, 2026 — the count-error protocol). Gate 4: PAR levels shift with the season. Capacitors in June are not capacitors in January. The restock loop in Step 4 resets min/max levels quarterly against usage history, or the "optimized" levels from last summer will starve you in the next heat wave. Gate 5: this is an inventory lane, not a tax lane. Shrinking on-hand value changes your balance sheet and year-end count obligations — run the plan past your accountant before a December purge, not after.

The verdict: the van is a warehouse that loses $220 every time the door opens empty

A service van is not storage — it is a rolling warehouse that dispenses revenue. When a tech finds the bin empty mid-job, the $20 capacitor costs far more than $20: a 60-minute supply-house run at a $175/hour billable rate burns $175 in lost top line, $15 in fuel, and $30 in technician wages — $220 in total cost for one missing part (repair-crm.com, June 2026). And the quiet leak is worse than the loud one: the small fittings, wire nuts, and lubricants that never make it onto the invoice can erase 5% to 10% of annual profit for a 1-to-5-person shop (same source, June 2026). This playbook builds a parts-ready van in five steps: cost the leak so the team feels it, bin and map every van to PAR levels, barcode-scan everything through a free app, run a weekly restock loop the office executes without meetings, and close the loop on first-time fix rate — so the truck that arrives finishes the job.

1Cost the leak — the stockout audit (Week 1)

You cannot fix a leak nobody priced. For 2 weeks, every tech logs 4 fields on a shared sheet when a part is missing: part, job, minutes burned (drive + wait), and whether the part got invoiced. The office totals it Friday: runs × $220 + unbilled-part value. A 3-tech shop losing 3–5 hours per tech per week to missing stock (Fieldproxy plumbing blueprint, crawled October 2026 — vendor ceiling, labeled) is burning roughly $1,000–$1,800 a week in lost billable time alone at that $175/hour rate. Post the weekly total on the shop whiteboard — the leak you can see is the leak the team will fix.

Tools: the shared sheet the shop already has. No software yet — software comes after the habit.

2Bin and map the van — PAR levels per truck (Week 2)

Strip one van to the floor and rebuild it around zones: high-velocity bins within arm's reach of the side door (capacitors, contactors, fuses, common fittings), medium-velocity mid-van (motors, boards, valves), seasonal/slow on the back shelf. Every bin gets a label with the part number, the bin location code (e.g. A-12), and its PAR pair: the reorder point and the max. Set the first PAR levels from the Step 1 audit, not from a catalog — your last 90 days of jobs know what your vans actually burn. Photograph the finished bin map and pin it inside the door: the map is the standard, and the standard is what a fill-in tech stocks to.

Tools: a label printer or a sheet of adhesive labels. Total outlay: under $30.

3Scan everything — the free barcode layer (Week 3)

The phone the tech already carries is the scanner. Sortly's free tier and MaintainX's free tier both offer barcode/QR-based tracking on the phone with low-stock alerts (facilio.com parts-inventory comparison, crawled October 2026 — verify tiers on vendor sites before buying). The rule is simple and binary: nothing leaves a bin without a scan at issue, nothing enters a van without a scan at restock. The office dashboard then shows live van counts per tech — and, critically, the unbilled-parts report: every part issued to a job that never landed on the invoice. That report alone closes the 5–10% profit leak from Step 1, because "inventory leakage" (repair-crm.com, June 2026) only survives where nobody reconciles.

Tools: Sortly free tier or MaintainX free tier (barcode/QR scanning, low-stock alerts); paid tiers start around $20–$24/user/month per the October 2026 comparison — the free tier runs this playbook.

4The weekly restock loop — no meetings, no memory (ongoing)

Friday, 15 minutes, one workflow: each tech scans the bins below their reorder point (the app flags them), the office consolidates the flags across vans into one purchase order, and Monday's scheduled jobs drive the pre-pull list — the parts each tech's day needs, staged before dispatch. Aberdeen's service-parts research (via sdcexec.com) found that shops coordinating parts planning across the network cut inventory levels 10–15% and halved stockouts versus shops planning locally — the weekly loop is that coordination, for a 5-van fleet. Add the tech-to-tech transfer rule: when a bin runs dry mid-day, the dashboard shows which nearby van holds the part, and the transfer scans out of one van and into the job — the emergency parts run becomes a 10-minute handoff (Fieldproxy vendor ceiling: 85% fewer emergency trips — labeled).

Tools: the barcode app's dashboard + the shop's existing sheet or a Make scenario that turns Friday's low-stock flags into one consolidated PO. Make was verified against its own affiliate and pricing pages October 1–2, 2026.

5Close the loop on first-time fix rate (monthly)

The scoreboard has two numbers: stockout incidents per month (target: fewer than 2 per month per the HVAC parts-tracking benchmark, crawled October 2026) and first-time fix rate. The Aquant 2026 Field Service Benchmark Report puts the gap between "hero" technicians and everyone else at 6.5 percentage points of first-time fix — and parts readiness is one of the few levers that moves the average tech toward hero territory (benchmark PDF via 21176235.hs-sites.com, 2026). When stockouts fall and fix rates rise, the ledger shows the payback: every avoided return visit is a slot the next billable job takes. Report the two numbers to the team monthly alongside the leak total from Step 1 — the loop only survives if the savings stay visible.

Tools: the app's monthly export + the Step 1 sheet. One dashboard, 5 minutes.

Re-skin check — why this is not playbook #20 wearing a new hat

None of the 22 prior playbooks touch inventory — every one is customer-contact, back-office, workforce, or content. The closest cousin is #18 (end-of-life equipment ledger): #18 tracks the customer's equipment and when it should be replaced — this tracks the shop's own parts and whether they're on the truck. Different object (your parts vs their machines), different trigger (a missing bin mid-job vs an aging unit), different metric (stockout incidents and first-time fix rate vs replacement pipeline). This is the family's first inventory playbook and its first warehouse-on-wheels system — the van as a stock location, not a vehicle.

Honest weak points

Stated up front, not in a footnote: (1) the vendor ceiling numbers (85% fewer trips, 95–98% accuracy) come from a seller of this exact software — the playbook treats them as the ceiling, and the $220/run and 5–10% leakage figures as the floor; (2) the #1 failure mode is behavior — techs who won't scan will beat any app, which is why Step 1 is an audit and Step 3 is a binary rule, not a suggestion; (3) PAR levels decay every season (Gate 4) — skip the quarterly reset and last summer's "optimal" van starves you in January; (4) the 6–12-month ramp — the first stockout-free month arrives before the habit is permanent.

Questions this page must answer

Won't techs hate scanning every part?

They hate the supply-house run more. The pitch that lands: this is 90 seconds at the end of the day to never drive 40 minutes for a capacitor again. Shops that frame it as "the app protects your paycheck from callbacks" (see #18's warranty math) get adoption faster than shops that frame it as management surveillance.

Do we need to buy a dedicated barcode scanner?

No. The phone the tech already carries is the scanner — Sortly and MaintainX both scan from the phone camera on their free tiers (facilio.com comparison, crawled October 2026). A dedicated scanner is a purchase; the playbook's budget is $0 plus labels.

How does this stop the unbilled-parts leak?

The scan-at-issue rule ties every part to a job number, and the weekly reconciliation flags every part issued to a job that never landed on the invoice. The 5–10% profit leak (repair-crm.com, June 2026) survives on "nobody reconciles" — the report ends that.

What about high-value parts we don't want on every van?

PAR max of 1 for high-value/low-velocity parts, or keep them in the office warehouse and let the Monday pre-pull list reserve them against scheduled jobs. The playbook's bin zones already split high-velocity (side door) from slow (back shelf) — expensive parts live in the slow zone with a tight reorder point.

How long until we see the money?

The first avoided supply-house run pays the labels off on day one. The measurable wins — fewer than 2 stockout incidents per month, first-time fix rate moving toward the top quartile — land in months 2–3 of the restock loop. The habit being permanent is the 6–12-month story; the math is immediate.
