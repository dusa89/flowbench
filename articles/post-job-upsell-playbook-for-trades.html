Playbook #11: The Post-Job Upsell Playbook for Home Services & Trades (2026)

The no-pressure gates: this system recommends, it never corners

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because an upsell system that relies on pressure is a reputation liability wearing a revenue mask:

  1. Show, price, pause. Every recommendation is presented with a photo of the actual condition, a written price, and a real pause for the homeowner to decide. No "sign now or the truck leaves" framing, no invented danger, no surprise fees after the yes.
  2. Decline = done, on-site. A "not today" ends the pitch permanently for that visit. The only permitted follow-up is the step-4 nurture — educational, capped, and off forever on "stop" or any complaint.
  3. Commission plans are disclosed to the tech, never the target. If you pay spiffs per add-on, train against quota-pressure: quality of recommendation first, volume second, and the monthly ledger (step 5) reviews complaint rate next to attach rate.

The short version

You already paid to win the lead, route the truck, and earn the homeowner's trust — the marginal cost of one more honest recommendation while you're standing in the home is close to zero, which makes ticket growth the highest-margin growth there is. The shops that run a real post-job upsell system: pre-load the tech with the home's service history and equipment age before arrival, present a digital multi-option proposal with photo evidence in the home (same-visit approvals convert roughly 3x better than callback quotes), and run a capped 2–3 message decline-nurture that recovers 15–20% of "not todays" within 90 days. Vendor-published automation blueprints (Fieldproxy, crawled October 2026) claim 38–45% upsell acceptance and $85–$185 average ticket lift per job in carpet-cleaning and septic workflows — treat vendor numbers as a ceiling, not a promise. The independent math that holds up: Contractor Magazine's long-running rule that a $30 average-ticket lift moves roughly $60,000 a year of bottom line for a typical four-plumber shop, and a trade-business math paper (JobOSPro, crawled July 2026) showing that upselling existing jobs runs 5–7x cheaper than acquiring a new customer. Run this playbook and the second ticket stops being a matter of which tech feels like selling.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0Recommend to your own family; price before the yes

Rule one: the tech recommends only what they would recommend to their own family in the same house. Rule two: every recommendation carries a price and a photo before the homeowner says yes. Post these in the shop and put them in the proposal template footer. Shops that skip the rules end up in the same reviews this page's sibling #6 (review capture) works so hard to earn.

Runs on: your field-service CRM (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber — any system with job history + equipment records) + a digital proposal tool. No AI required for the core loop; AI earns its keep in steps 2–4.

Step 1 — Fire the 48-hour window trigger on job completion

1Job-completed webhook starts the clock

The moment the tech closes the job in the dispatch board, the automation creates an "upsell window" record: 48 hours on-site-adjacent plus a 14-day decline-nurture tail. The record carries the job type, equipment touched, home's service history, and a flag for any condition the tech photographed on-site. This is the same CRM-webhook event family that drives #6's review ask and #10's dormancy clock — but where #6 fires the sentiment check and #10 starts a 90-day inactivity timer, this trigger starts a 48-hour offer window. Three different systems, one event, three different jobs.

Step 2 — The on-site opportunity brief: the tech walks in knowing

2Pre-loaded candidates on the tablet, before the knock

Before arrival, the tech gets a one-screen brief: the home's job history, equipment age (a 14-year-old condenser, a 9-year-old water heater), past declined quotes, and 3–5 standard add-on candidates priced against your current pricebook. This is the whole trick: the best upselling shops don't depend on techs remembering to sell — the system identifies the candidates and the tech just confirms the diagnosis in person. A Septic workflow blueprint (Fieldproxy, crawled October 2026) describes this as running tank age, last inspection, and local code against a template library before the visit — presented there as a vendor claim, but the mechanism (pre-visit candidate generation) is independently sound. On-site, the tech adds anything they see with their own eyes: the photo of the failing capacitor, the galvanized nipple on the new heater, the 40-year-old panel. Candidates confirmed in person become proposals; candidates not confirmed die quietly — no quota, no forced list.

Step 3 — The same-visit digital proposal with photo evidence

3Multi-option proposal, in the home, before the truck rolls

The confirmed candidates go into a digital proposal the tech builds at the kitchen table: good/better/best options where it makes sense, the photo of the condition attached to each line, flat-rate pricing, one-tap homeowner approval. The speed of this proposal is the conversion engine: Fieldproxy's well-pump blueprint (crawled October 2026, vendor claim) reports on-site proposals converting roughly 3x better than quotes that go out 24–48 hours later — and a September 2026 TechSee survey of 1,500 US adults (reported via TipRanks) backs the mechanism independently: 67% of respondents trust a technician's recommendation more when they can see the problem. The photo does the selling; the tech does the explaining. Accepted add-ons get scheduled or completed same-visit; the office never calls back to quote — the whole point is that the callback quote is where add-on revenue goes to die.

Step 4 — The decline nurture: 15–20% of "not today" is still on the table

4Two to three educational touches, then off forever

Declined offers are not dead leads — they're leads with a reason. Within 48 hours the homeowner gets one educational touch (the photo from the visit, what it means, what it costs to wait), around day 7 a second (seasonal timing or a bundle note), and nothing after day 14. Any "stop," any complaint, any booking kills the sequence across all channels. Fieldproxy's septic blueprint (crawled October 2026, vendor claim) puts the decline-nurture recovery rate at 15–20% within 90 days — plausible as a ceiling for shops that follow the photo-first, no-pressure rules from the hard box. Every touch carries the shop's name and a human reply path; this is follow-up, not spam.

Step 5 — The prevention loop: measure the leak you just plugged

5The per-tech attach-rate ledger, reviewed monthly

Every offer lives in the ledger: candidate presented, accepted, declined, nurture-recovered. Monthly, the owner reviews three numbers per tech: attach rate (accepted ÷ presented), average ticket lift, and complaint rate. The complaint rate is the guardrail — a tech with a high attach rate and rising complaints is selling, not recommending. The third number is the one that was always missing: the never-presented report — jobs where the brief had candidates and none were offered. That's the leak this playbook exists to close, and it's the one metric that distinguishes a system from a hope. Fieldproxy's carpet-cleaning blueprint (crawled October 2026, vendor claim) frames systematic identification as eliminating $40,000–$80,000 in annual revenue lost to forgotten upsells — the number is vendor marketing, but the audit it implies (count the opportunities you never voiced) is the real work.

Common objections, answered honestly

Isn't this just upselling? My customers will hate it.

Customers hate being sold to; they don't hate being shown a real problem with a real price. The TechSee September 2026 survey (1,500 US adults, reported via TipRanks) found 67% trust a recommendation more when they can see the problem — the photo does the trust work that a pitch never could. The hard box at the top of this page is the whole difference: show, price, pause, and a decline that stays declined.

My techs aren't salespeople. Can this work anyway?

That is exactly the premise. The brief (step 2) does the remembering; the photo (step 3) does the persuading; the tech does what techs do — diagnose and explain. Shops fail at this when they ask techs to memorize add-ons and improvise pitches. Systems beat memory every time.

How much can this realistically add per job?

Anchor to independent math, not vendor claims. Contractor Magazine's long-running rule: a $30 average-ticket lift moves roughly $60,000 a year for a typical four-plumber shop. BDR's 2026 plumbing trends report notes high-performing residential shops holding $300–$500 average tickets through consistent upselling and memberships. Vendor automation blueprints (Fieldproxy, crawled October 2026) claim $85–$185 per job — treat those as the ceiling your system is working toward, not the floor it starts on.

Won't this slow down my techs and cost me jobs per day?

The proposal takes minutes at the kitchen table, and it replaces the office callback quote — the step that already costs you the add-on. BDR's 2026 plumbing data frames the tradeoff shops actually face: high performers protect average ticket and throughput. Start with the 48-hour window only (steps 1–3), add the nurture (step 4) once the offer habit is solid, and let the ledger (step 5) tell you whether the ticket lift is paying for any extra minutes on-site.

Does the decline nurture annoy people?

Capped at two to three educational touches, killed by any "stop," any complaint, or any booking, it reads as follow-through, not pressure. The shops that get complaints are the ones that never stop — which is why the off-forever rule sits in the hard box, before step 0. First-party lists, quiet hours, and AI self-ID still apply from the family template (see #10's gates) anywhere you automate the outreach.
