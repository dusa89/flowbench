The blitz honesty gates: a pressure campaign is a list-burner without these

A seasonal blitz is the one playbook in this family where the pressure itself is the product — which is exactly why the gates sit before step 0. Gate 1 — consent on file, opt-out in every message, quiet hours: every text carries a working opt-out (reply STOP), one opt-out kills the whole blitz for that contact, and no automated message goes out before 8am or after 9pm in the contact's local time. The blitz touches your whole list — including current, happy customers — so a consent miss here poisons relationships you already own, not strangers. Gate 2 — never bluff the deadline: the AI voice agent never invents a deadline to create urgency. The seasonal deadline is real — first freeze, National Fall Tune-Up Day (observed September 25) — and the script says only true things about it. A bluffed deadline that a customer catches is a one-star review, not a booking. Gate 3 — never sell a slot you can't staff: every booking checks live capacity before it confirms (step 3's surge guard). A blitz that books 60 tune-ups into 40 slots is a dispatch fire that cancels on customers during the week you were supposed to impress them. Gate 4 — no guilt, no shame: the pitch is value and timing ("your furnace is due, before wait times spike"), never fear ("your family will freeze") or guilt about missed maintenance. Scrub every send against your do-not-contact list before it fires. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice — run your consent language and scripts past counsel for your state.

The short version

Our reactivation playbook (#10) works the customers who went quiet with a 21-day dormancy-ladder sequence. This works the whole list — active, dormant, plan members — with a 14-day seasonal compression campaign that fills the shoulder weeks before peak season hits. The job is filling empty slow-season capacity: a 2–3 week blitz, staggered message drops across the database, two-way booking that checks live capacity before it confirms, geographic clustering so techs can run 9–11 tune-ups a day, and the at-visit conversion into maintenance plans and repair work. The demand case is the calendar itself: HomeServe's September 2026 survey of HVAC contractors found 43% say most customers don't proactively schedule a tune-up (BusinessWire, September 21, 2026) — the work is sitting in your database, unasked-for. HomeServe's 2026 State of the Home survey found 22% of American homeowners needed an HVAC repair or replacement in the past 12 months, and more than half have $1,000 or less set aside for unexpected repairs — so the tune-up is the affordable visit that finds the expensive problem first. A professional furnace tune-up costs $150–$300 on average and takes about an hour; the repair it prevents starts with a $75–$200 service call fee plus hourly and parts, and a full replacement runs $3,800–$12,000 (HomeServe, September 2026). Maintenance cuts heating costs 5–10%, and heating is nearly half of a home's energy use. The seasonal argument writes itself: HomeServe's contractor poll found 18% of contractors had moderate or extended wait times during peak heating season, with 11% reporting 3+ day waits or having to turn customers away (same release) — book the tune-up now or join the queue later. The build is Make (the staggered drops, the capacity guard, CRM sync), Synthflow (the AI booking call for non-responders), ElevenLabs voices so the call sounds like your office, and your field-service CRM's live schedule as the source of truth. Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The deadline is real, and capacity comes before the campaign

Rule 1 — pick the real deadline before you pick the list. A blitz without a deadline is a discount blast, and discount blasts train customers to wait. The deadline must be a real calendar event: first freeze, National Fall Tune-Up Day (September 25), the first predicted cold snap. The script states the deadline and what it means (wait times spike, 18% of contractors were already backed up at peak per the HomeServe poll) — never an invented countdown. Rule 2 — build the capacity before you create the demand. Pre-block the blitz weeks in the schedule first: dedicated tune-up blocks, the surge ceiling (how many tune-ups per day per tech before quality drops), and the overflow rule (what happens when demand beats the blocks — waitlist with a promised date, not a booked slot that can't be staffed). A blitz is a demand surge you engineer on purpose; engineering a surge you can't absorb is the most expensive way to make customers distrust your reminders. The sequence below assumes the blocks exist — capacity is the price of admission, not step 5.

Step 1 — The whole-list sweep: active, dormant, plan members, all of it

1Export the full database and segment by last seasonal service, not just dormancy

Unlike #10 — which works only the quiet segments — the blitz touches the whole database, because a furnace that hasn't been tuned this season is due whether the customer is a stranger, a regular, or a plan member. Pull the full customer list from the CRM and segment by last seasonal service, not by days since last contact: (a) due this season (no fall tune-up on record), (b) serviced last fall only (prime upsell to a plan), (c) plan members (their visit is pre-scheduled — they get a confirmation, not a pitch), (d) never had a tune-up (new-system owners from spring installs — the highest repair-discovery segment). Scrub every segment for consent and the do-not-contact list first — the blitz is the largest send of the year, so a hygiene miss here is the costliest. Then rank each segment by expected value: segment (b) converts to plans, segment (d) finds the most repair work, segment (a) fills the blocks. The drop order follows the rank.

Runs on: Make (CRM export, segment tagging) + your field-service CRM's service history as the source of truth.

Step 2 — The compression launch: staggered drops over 10–14 days, never one blast

2Drops land in ranked order; every message opens two-way and ends at a booking

One blast to 4,000 contacts books 400 tune-ups into 200 slots and breaks dispatch by Tuesday. The blitz runs staggered drops over 10–14 days: highest-value segment first, next segment when the blocks are 70% full, and the surge guard (step 3) gates every send on remaining capacity — a drop that would overbook its week pauses automatically and its contacts wait for the next wave. Each drop is a two-way SMS: the seasonal hook ("fall tune-ups are open — book before the first freeze, wait times spike once it hits"), the one-line value ("$150–300 now, or a $75–200 service call in January"), and the self-serve booking link with live capacity behind it. Non-responders get the AI call on day 3 — Synthflow, identified per the gate, booking into the same live schedule. The message is a seasonal service reminder, not a discount: the blitz sells timing and availability, and the price stays the price, because a blitz that trains customers to wait for "blitz pricing" is a margin cut wearing a costume.

Runs on: Make (drop scheduling, capacity-gated send logic, reply routing) + Synthflow (the day-3 AI booking call) + ElevenLabs (voice).

Step 3 — The surge guard: every booking checks live capacity before it confirms

3No booking confirms without a real slot; geography clusters the days

This is the machinery that makes the blitz a system instead of a message blast — and the piece the seasonal-reminder-as-message has no answer for. Every booking path — the self-serve link, the two-way SMS reply, the AI call — queries live capacity before it confirms: date, window, tech, and the pre-blocked tune-up ceiling for that day. If the day is full, the customer gets the next real date, not a slot that doesn't exist. Meanwhile the schedule clusters by geography: tune-ups are the one job type where 9–11 stops per tech per day is realistic (Fieldproxy vendor claim, October 2026 — vs 6–7 for reactive scheduling), but only if the route is tight. The guard holds two per-day ceilings: the hard ceiling (staff limit — never exceeded) and the soft ceiling (the quality limit — beyond it, the next drops pause and the overflow waitlist opens with a promised contact date). Overbooked-and-canceled is worse than never-booked: the blitz's trust depends on every confirmed visit happening exactly when promised.

Runs on: Make (live schedule query, ceiling logic, overflow routing) + your field-service CRM's calendar as the source of truth.

Step 4 — The at-visit conversion: the tune-up is the funnel, not the finish

4Digital inspection with photo evidence feeds the upsell; the next season books before the tech leaves

The blitz pays for itself twice: once on the tune-up revenue, and again on what the visit uncovers. The tech runs a digital inspection with photo documentation on every blitz tune-up — the photos are the trust mechanism for recommended repairs and upgrades (Fieldproxy's vendor blueprint claims 40% higher approval rates on recommended work with digital photo workflows — vendor claim, October 2026). The playbook's sibling #11 (post-job upsell) owns the on-site offer mechanics; this page's job is making sure every blitz visit gets them: the pre-visit briefing includes the customer's system history, and the tech's checklist ends with two questions — the plan offer (Fieldproxy claims 42% of tune-up visits convert to maintenance plans — vendor claim) and the next-season booking ("your AC tune-up is due in April — want me to lock the slot now?"). Plan sign-ups flow straight to #8's enrollment engine. A blitz tune-up that leaves without a plan offer and without the spring booking is revenue that rented the truck for a day and left the future behind.

Runs on: the tech's inspection app + #11's same-visit proposal flow + #8's enrollment automation; Make logs plan conversions to the blitz ledger.

Step 5 — The blitz ledger: capacity fill, segment yield, plan conversion

5Track the blitz as a campaign, not as messages — then run it twice a year

Log every blitz as one campaign record: total outreach, bookings by segment (a/b/c/d), booking rate, capacity fill % on the blitz blocks, plan conversions, repair/replace attach from blitz tune-ups, and revenue per blitz week. The metrics that matter: capacity fill (did the surge get absorbed or did it break dispatch?), segment yield (which segment paid best — reorder next blitz's drop order), and plan conversion (the compounding return). The blitz runs twice a year — fall heating tune-ups and spring cooling tune-ups — so the ledger is what makes the second one better than the first: dead segments get dropped, the winning segments get earlier drops, the ceilings get tuned to what dispatch actually handled. A blitz you run once is a stunt; a blitz you ledger twice a year is a revenue system.

Runs on: Make (campaign event logging) + a one-page owner report after the blitz closes; plan conversions sync to the CRM.

The re-skin check — why this is its own playbook

#10 reactivates the quiet with a 21-day sequence; the killed seasonal-reminder was a message inside it; #13 fills the shoulder weeks with a 14-day whole-list compression. Three different jobs: #10's trigger is customer dormancy (the monthly CRM scan finds who went quiet, then a 21-day SMS/email/call sequence wins them back) — its population is the quiet segments only. The seasonal-reminder candidate was killed as a re-skin because it was a single message type living inside #10's sequence — a message is not a system. This page's trigger is the calendar: the seasonal deadline (first freeze, September 25 Fall Tune-Up Day), and its population is the whole database, including happy current customers and plan members — people #10 deliberately ignores. Different skill: this is demand compression with a surge guard (staggered drops, live capacity checks, overflow ceilings), not a nurture sequence. Different metric: capacity fill and revenue per blitz week, not reactivation rate. The hooks are clean too: the at-visit conversion hands its upsell mechanics to #11 (post-job upsell) and its plan sign-ups to #8 (plan enrollment) — this page ends at the blitz ledger. A page that tried to do the visit itself would swallow both siblings.

Frequently asked questions

I'm a two-truck shop. Is this overkill?

The honest read: for a two-truck shop, the surge guard is a whiteboard, not a build — block the tune-up weeks on the wall calendar, cap bookings at what two techs can actually run, and send the SMS drops from the CRM you already pay for. Automate the drop scheduling and the confirmation replies first (steps 1–2 — the cheapest to run and the easiest to forget by hand), and build the live capacity guard only when a blitz starts overbooking you. The two-truck version of this playbook is discipline plus a calendar; the automation comes when the volume demands it.

Isn't this just spamming my whole customer list?

A spam blast is untargeted, unconsented, and offers nothing. The blitz is the opposite on all three: every contact has consent on file and an opt-out in every message (gate 1), the segments are ranked by actual service history (step 1), and the offer is a real seasonal service with a real deadline — the thing the customer will pay more for in January when wait times spike. Pressure without value is spam; a seasonal deadline with a service behind it is the business doing its job.

Fall or spring — which blitz do I run?

Both, but fall first. Heating has the stronger fear-avoidance pull (safety, freeze damage, the HomeServe stat that 43% of contractors' customers never book proactively) and the harder deadline. Spring's AC tune-up blitz is the same system with a different deadline and a lighter urgency — and the fall ledger tells you exactly which segments to reorder for spring. Plan members get a confirmation, not a pitch, in both.

What if the blitz overbooks and dispatch melts down?

That's what step 3's two ceilings are for: the soft ceiling pauses the remaining drops and opens the overflow waitlist with a promised contact date, and the hard ceiling (staff limit) is never exceeded. Overflow customers get a human call within 24 hours with two real alternates — the same save mechanics as #12's save sequence. And the first blitz runs with conservative ceilings on purpose: the ledger from run one calibrates run two. An overbooked blitz is a one-time error; a blitz with no ceilings is a policy.

How is this different from playbook #10 (dormant-customer reactivation)?

#10 is a rifle: it finds the quiet segments with a monthly dormancy scan and works them with a 21-day sequence until they rebook — metric: reactivation rate. #13 is a shotgun with a timer: it works the whole list in 14 days against a seasonal deadline and its hard problem is absorbing the demand surge without breaking dispatch — metric: capacity fill. Dormant customers appear in both, but #10's dormant customer gets a personalized 21-day sequence and #13's gets a seasonal booking slot with everyone else. If a blitz needs a segment-specific win-back inside it, that's #10's sequence, invoked — not duplicated.
