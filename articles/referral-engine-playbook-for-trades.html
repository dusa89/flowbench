The referral honesty gates: rewards are a trust product, and trust breaks easily

A referral program is the one playbook in this family that pays customers for speech about your business — which is exactly why the gates sit before step 0. Gate 1 — never condition a reward on a positive review: the referral ask and the review ask are two different conversations (our review-capture sibling, #6, owns the review ask, and it asks for the truth, good or bad). The FTC's Endorsement Guides treat a reward conditioned on a positive review as deceptive — the referral reward pays for the introduction, and the friend's business is earned on the work, never bought. Gate 2 — reward the introduction, not a story: the reward is two-sided and disclosed — the friend gets $50 off their first job, the referrer gets a $50 account credit — and both sides see the terms in plain language before anyone shares anything. Hidden mechanics or clawback fine print turn advocates into ex-customers. Gate 3 — only satisfied customers get the ask: the referral invitation goes only to customers who already gave a satisfaction signal (a 5-star review, an accepted upsell, a renewed plan — step 1). Asking a detractor for referrals is how a reward program becomes a megaphone for complaints. Gate 4 — consent, opt-out, and identity: every referral touch carries a working opt-out (reply STOP), quiet hours apply, and any AI voice thank-you identifies itself in the first sentence. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice — run your reward terms and scripts past counsel for your state.

The short version

Our review-capture playbook (#6) turns completed jobs into public reviews. Our post-job upsell (#11) sells more work to the same customer in the 48-hour window. This playbook turns the same satisfied customers into named introductions: the referral engine. The job is manufacturing what every contractor already wants and almost nobody systematizes — customers who bring customers: a satisfied-signal trigger that picks exactly who gets the ask, a one-tap two-sided invitation the customer can forward in seconds, referral attribution in the CRM so the shop knows whose introductions book, and a thank-you loop that makes advocates refer again. The demand case is the trade's own buying behavior: a 2026 Roofing Contractor and Owens Corning homeowner survey found 74% of homeowners would follow a recommendation from a neighbor, friend, or family member (via Profit Acuity's September 29, 2026 contractor-leads report) — and that same report found referrals remain contractors' top source of new business, with more than 60% of referred homeowners researching the contractor online before they call. The quality case is settled: Wharton's long-running study found referred customers deliver 16% higher lifetime value and churn 18% less (via Extole's 2026 referral roundup), Nielsen's Global Trust research found 88% of consumers trust recommendations from people they know above all other advertising, and Extole's 2026 Referral Index reports referred customers convert at up to 5x the rate of cold paid traffic. The mechanics favor two-sided rewards — Friendbuy's benchmarks find they outperform one-sided rewards by roughly 2x in share rate — and the honest baseline is Extole's 2026 finding that the median advocacy rate across 400 referral programs sits at around 19%, with the top 20% of advocates driving most of it. The build is Make (the trigger logic, the share-link generation, CRM attribution, the re-ask schedule), Synthflow (the personal AI thank-you call when a referral books), ElevenLabs voices so the call sounds like your office, and your CRM's customer record as the source of truth. All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0Only satisfied customers get the ask, and the reward is a two-sided credit

Rule 1 — the ask follows the satisfaction signal, never the job completion alone. A completed job is not proof of a happy customer; an unhappy customer who just paid is the last person who should get a referral invitation. The trigger for step 1 is one of four satisfaction signals: a 5-star review posted (#6's output), an accepted upsell (#11's output), a plan-member renewal (#8), or a 9–10 on the job-complete survey. No signal, no ask — the automation's first job is not asking. Rule 2 — the reward is a two-sided account credit, sized against a job ticket. Two-sided ($50 off the friend's first job + a $50 credit for the referrer) beats one-sided by about 2x in share rate (Friendbuy benchmarks), because the referrer isn't asking a favor — they're giving a gift. Size it against your average ticket: $50 on a $300 repair is generous; $50 on a $12,000 replacement is a rounding error. The terms state the credit in plain language, what it applies to, and that it arrives only when the referred friend books — a reward for the introduction that books, not for names submitted. The sequence below assumes the two rules are written and the reward terms are live in the CRM — the terms are the price of admission, not step 5.

Step 1 — The satisfied-signal trigger: pick exactly who gets the ask

1Four signals, one entry point — and the detractors never enter

The trigger fires on whichever satisfaction signal lands first: (a) a 5-star review posts on Google — #6's check-in already identified this customer as happy, so the ask arrives within 24 hours while the glow is fresh; (b) the customer accepts a #11 upsell — an upsell buyer just voted with their wallet; (c) a #8 plan member renews — renewal is the strongest loyalty signal a trades shop has; (d) a 9–10 score on the job-complete survey. The CRM tags the customer "referral-eligible" and suppresses the ask for anyone with an open complaint, a refund in flight, or an overdue invoice (#9's bucket — a customer you're collecting from gets no referral invitation, ever). This is the boundary #6 doesn't cross: #6 asks every customer for an honest review; this page asks only the satisfied for an introduction. The entry list is small on purpose — a referral program that asks everyone is a discount program wearing a costume.

Runs on: Make (signal listeners on the review webhook, the CRM upsell flag, the plan-renewal event, the survey score) + your CRM's customer record as the source of truth.

Step 2 — The one-tap ask: a two-sided invitation the customer can forward in seconds

2Day-3 personal share link + a pre-written forward, not a flyer

Three days after the satisfaction signal, the customer gets one personal message: a two-way SMS (or email if the channel prefers) with their unique share link, a one-tap pre-written forward they can send to a neighbor — "Here's the crew that did our AC — $50 off your first job if you mention this link" — and the reward terms in two lines. The design principle: the customer does one tap, not homework. No PDFs to attach, no codes to memorize, no "tell them to mention your name" (names get lost at the front desk — this is why step 3's attribution exists). Non-responders get exactly one nudge at day 14 — the "your link is still live" reminder — and then silence until step 4's quarterly re-ask. An advocate is not a subscriber list: the program touches them four times a year, not forty.

Runs on: Make (link generation, day-3 send, day-14 nudge, suppression logic) + your CRM's contact record for the unique advocate ID.

Step 3 — The attribution and thank-you loop: know whose introductions book

3Every referral lands in the CRM with the advocate attached; the thank-you is personal

This is the machinery that makes the engine a system instead of a "mention my name" hope — and the piece the verbal-ask tradition has no answer for. Every share link is unique per advocate, and every booking that arrives through one writes two CRM records: the new lead tagged with its source advocate, and the advocate's record incremented with a booked referral. When the referred friend's job completes, two things fire: the $50 credit posts to both accounts with a plain-language notification, and the advocate gets a personal thank-you — an AI voice call from the office (Synthflow, identified per the gate) or a hand-written-text SMS from the owner on smaller books — naming the friend's first name and the outcome. Wharton's finding that referred customers are 4x more likely to refer others (via ReferralCandy's 2026 roundup) is why the loop closes on the friend too: 30 days after their own completed job, they get a satisfaction signal check, and their own advocate invitation. The referral that compounds is the one that gets thanked by name.

Runs on: Make (link-to-booking attribution, credit posting, thank-you triggers) + Synthflow (the booked-referral thank-you call) + ElevenLabs (voice) + your CRM as the ledger.

Step 4 — The re-ask: advocates get quarterly, plan members get the annual recap

4Four touches a year, each one carrying proof of what the program did

Referral programs die because the shop asks once and never again. The engine re-asks on a calendar: every quarter, active advocates get one message — their personal stats ("you've referred 3 neighbors; 2 booked; $100 in credits earned") plus a fresh share link for the season ahead. Plan members (#8) get the annual referral recap at renewal: "your referrals brought us 4 jobs this year — here's $200 in credits and your link for next year." Referred customers who became customers get their own advocate invitation per step 3 — the second generation is where Extole's compounding finding (referred customers up to 5x more likely to use the referral program) shows up in the ledger. Each re-ask is suppression-aware: advocates who opted out stay out, and the day-14 nudge non-responders from step 2 re-enter only at the quarterly cadence. An advocate who unsubscribed from the program still gets service, still gets #6's review ask, still gets #10's win-back if they go quiet — the referral lane is one lane, not the whole relationship.

Runs on: Make (quarterly advocate sweep, stat-block assembly, renewal-recap trigger) + your CRM's advocate segment.

Step 5 — The referral ledger: referrals per 100 jobs, booking rate, real CAC

5Track the engine as an acquisition channel, not as goodwill

Log every quarter as one channel record: referrals generated per 100 completed jobs (the ask rate and the share rate), referral-to-booking conversion (share link → booked job), referral CAC (total credits paid ÷ referred jobs booked) versus your paid-channel CAC, average ticket of referred jobs (Wharton: 16% higher LTV — check it on your own book), and advocate repeat rate (what share of advocates refer twice). The metrics that matter: share rate (is the ask working — Extole's ~19% median advocacy rate is your benchmark), booking conversion (are the introductions qualified — neighbors of happy customers usually are), and referral CAC vs paid CAC (the engine wins when a $100 two-sided credit beats a $150+ paid lead). The hooks are clean: a referred lead that goes dark on its estimate feeds #4's 30-day quote-chase with its advocate tag intact — the follow-up can honestly say "your neighbor Mike suggested us." A referral engine you ledger quarterly is an acquisition channel; one you never measure is a prayer.

Runs on: Make (quarterly channel report assembly) + a one-page owner report; referred estimates sync to #4's chase with the advocate tag.

The re-skin check — why this is its own playbook

#6 captures reviews; #11 sells more to the same customer; #14 manufactures named introductions from satisfied customers. Three different jobs. #6's trigger is job completion and its population is every customer — its output is public credibility (a Google review), and it deliberately asks for the truth, good or bad, with complaints routed to the owner first. This page's trigger is a satisfaction signal (a 5-star review, an accepted upsell, a plan renewal) and its population is only the proven happy — its output is a named new lead with a $50 reason to call. #11's job ends at the same customer's wallet in the 48-hour post-job window; this page's job starts at the customer's address book and compounds over quarters. Different skill too: this is reward economics (two-sided vs one-sided, sized to the ticket), referral attribution (link-to-booking matching in the CRM), and advocate lifecycle (the quarterly re-ask) — none of which appear in #6's reputation mechanics or #11's on-site proposal flow. The hooks are clean as well: #6's 5-star output feeds this page's trigger, #11's accepted upsells and #8's renewals are the richest advocate segments, and referred leads that ghost on estimates hand off to #4's quote-chase with the advocate tag intact — this page ends at the referral ledger. A page that tried to do reviews or upsells inside it would swallow two siblings.

Frequently asked questions

I'm a two-truck shop. Is this overkill?

The honest read: for a two-truck shop, the engine is a spreadsheet and a habit, not a build — a shared sheet with advocate names, referral links as simple CRM notes, the $50 two-sided credit applied by hand, and the owner making the thank-you call personally (which is better than any AI voice at this size). Automate the day-3 ask and the link generation first (steps 1–2 — the parts a busy shop forgets), and build the attribution matching only when hand-tracking starts producing wrong credits. The two-truck version of this playbook is discipline plus a thank-you call; the automation comes when the volume demands it.

Isn't paying customers for referrals just bribing them?

A bribe buys a story; this pays for an introduction. The reward lands only when the referred friend books and completes a job — the shop pays for a real new customer at a real CAC, which is cheaper than most paid leads. The honesty gates are what keep it clean: the reward is never conditioned on a positive review (gate 1 — that's the FTC line), the terms are disclosed to both sides up front (gate 2), and only satisfied customers get the ask (gate 3). A program with hidden terms or review-conditioned rewards is a bribe with paperwork; a disclosed two-sided credit is an acquisition cost.

What size should the reward be?

Size it against your average ticket and your paid-channel CAC. The rule: the two-sided credit should be big enough to feel like a gift and small enough that referral CAC stays under paid CAC. $50 each way works on $250–$600 tickets (repairs, tune-ups); on $8,000–$15,000 tickets (replacements, remodels) it reads as trivial — scale to $100–$250 each way and check the referral CAC against what you pay for a booked estimate from ads. Friendbuy's ~2x share-rate advantage for two-sided over one-sided rewards is why both sides get paid, not just the referrer. And the credit beats cash: credits keep the value inside your business and avoid turning advocates into bounty hunters.

What if nobody shares — my customers are happy but quiet?

Then the ask is broken, not the customers. The three usual failures: the ask arrived with no satisfaction signal behind it (asking everyone — fix: step 1's gating), the forward took more than one tap (fix: the pre-written forward in step 2), or the reward was one-sided (fix: the Friendbuy two-sided finding). The benchmark is Extole's ~19% median advocacy rate across 400 programs — if your share rate sits under 10% with the gates and one-tap forward in place, the problem is usually the timing (the day-3 ask vs a dead CRM) or the offer (a $25 credit on a $500 ticket). Measure share rate in step 5's ledger and tune the ask, not the customers.

How is this different from playbook #6 (review capture)?

#6 is reputation: it asks every customer for an honest review right after the job, routes complaints to the owner first, and its output is public proof on Google. #14 is acquisition: it asks only satisfied customers for named introductions, pays a disclosed two-sided credit when the introduction books, and its output is new jobs in the CRM. #6's 5-star output feeds this page's trigger — the review is the satisfaction signal the referral ask waits for. If a program asked everyone for referrals and paid for reviews, it would be both ineffective and an FTC problem; the split is the whole design.
