Playbook #6: The Post-Job Review Capture Playbook for Home Services (2026)

The constraints: no review gating, ever — and the check-in call gets consent at booking

Three hard gates, and they sit before step 0 because violating them is the fastest way to lose a Google Business Profile. Gate 1 — never gate the ask: Google's prohibited-content policy forbids "discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, or selectively soliciting positive reviews from customers" (its fake-engagement clause). Asking only happy customers for reviews is gating — whether it happens in software or in conversation. The FTC's Consumer Review Rule (16 CFR Part 465, in force October 21, 2024) covers suppressing negative reviews, with civil penalties up to $53,088 per violation and a $4.2 million precedent against a retailer that hid bad reviews (reported by Cloutly, September 2026, citing the FTC). This playbook's design rule is absolute: every completed job gets the same review link on the same trigger. An unhappy customer is routed to the owner for private recovery — never blocked from the review link. Recovery is not suppression. Gate 2 — call consent: the AI check-in call is automated outreach to a real customer. Consent is captured at job booking ("we may call or text after the job to check the work") and recorded in the CRM before step 1 fires. Gate 3 — the AI never writes as you: replies to reviews are drafted by the AI and posted by the owner. Nothing in this playbook writes a review, posts a reply unattended, or fabricates a customer voice. This page is a playbook, not compliance advice.

The short version

Our contractor missed-call playbook (#1) catches the calls you miss — this playbook harvests the jobs you finish. A tech closes a job in the dispatch board, an AI voice agent calls the customer within hours, asks one question ("was everything handled right?"), and branches: happy customers get a text with the Google review link while the goodwill is fresh; unhappy ones get the owner on the line before a word goes public. Every customer gets the same link — gate 1 above is the law, not a suggestion. The demand case is consumer behavior measured this year: 97% of consumers read reviews for local businesses and 41% "always" read them (up from 29% in 2025); 68% require at least 4.0 stars before they'll use a business (up from 55% in 2025); 31% will only use 4.5 stars or higher; 47% won't consider a business with fewer than 20 reviews; and 74% want reviews written in the last three months (BrightLocal, Local Consumer Review Survey 2026, published February 11, 2026, panel of 1,002 US adults). Google is still the biggest single stop, used by 71% of review readers (same survey). And reviews are a ranking input, not just a trust badge: review quantity, recency, and sustained influx hold three top-fifteen slots in local ranking-factor compilations, and one retest of eight electrician listings found listings "exploded" on hitting ten reviews from nine — while steady monthly solicitation beats quarterly bursts (compiled local-SEO reference, crawled October 2026). The build is Make (the job-completed trigger and the unhappy-customer owner alert), Synthflow (the AI check-in call — sentiment split, review-link SMS branch), and ElevenLabs voices so the call sounds like your office. All stats above are third-party and dated — direction, not a guarantee for your market.

Step 0 — Write the two rules before you automate anything

0The consent line and the no-gating line, on paper

Before any tool is bought, the owner writes two sentences into the job paperwork. Sentence one (consent): "After your job, we may call or text to check the work and ask for a review — reply STOP to opt out." It goes in the booking confirmation and the CRM records the yes/no before anything fires. Sentence two (no gating): "Every completed job gets the same review request, happy or not." Print it where the office staff can see it, because the whole value of this playbook is the unhappy-customer path — a tech who "forgets" the call for a job they know went badly is gating by hand. The AI handles that path automatically, which is exactly why shops buy this instead of asking techs to remember.

Built with: the booking confirmation template + one CRM checkbox. No software yet — the rules ARE the product until step 1.

Step 1 — The job-completed trigger

1Dispatch board to automation, same day

When a tech marks the job complete in the dispatch board (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber — whatever the shop runs), a webhook fires into the automation. The trigger waits a short, configurable delay — same-day afternoon for morning jobs, next-morning for evening jobs — so the customer has lived with the finished work but the goodwill is still warm. The payload carries three fields: customer name, phone, and the job record (tech name, service performed, consent flag from step 0). If the consent flag is no or missing, the sequence stops here — silently. No consent, no call; that rule is load-bearing.

Built with: Make — the dispatch-board webhook, the consent gate, the delay, and the routing in step 2. This is the same glue the family uses everywhere (see the n8n vs Make comparison if the shop already runs n8n).

Step 2 — The AI check-in call

2One question, two branches

The voice agent calls and does exactly three things: identifies itself as the shop's follow-up line, thanks the customer, and asks one question — "Was everything handled right with today's [service]?" It does not pitch, upsell, or collect a star rating on the call. Branch A (happy): the call ends warm and the system texts the Google review link within the minute — short link, first name, nothing else. Branch B (anything but happy): the agent apologizes, asks if the owner can call back within the hour, and the automation opens an owner-callback task with the customer's words attached. No review link is withheld from branch B — the owner simply gets there first with the fix. The sentiment split is why this playbook exists: a text-only ask sends the review link into a complaint as happily as into a compliment. The AI call is the filter a text can't be.

Built with: Synthflow (the check-in call, sentiment branch, SMS branch) + ElevenLabs (the voice — a warm office voice, never a chipper robot) + Make (the owner-callback task on branch B). Keep the script under 45 seconds.

Step 3 — The review-link text

3Same link, everyone, no incentives

The text carries the shop's Google review link — the one from the Business Profile dashboard — and nothing else persuasive: no "leave us 5 stars," no discount for reviewing, no gift cards. Google's policy bans incentives tied to reviews outright, and requesting specific star ratings is exactly the "selectively soliciting" the policy forbids. One honest note on Yelp: Yelp's own guidance discourages asking for reviews at all, so this playbook's ask targets Google only — the page a future customer actually checks (71% of review readers, per the BrightLocal 2026 survey). If the customer doesn't click in 48 hours, one reminder text goes out — a single reminder, not a drip. Annoyance is a review too.

Built with: the Synthflow SMS branch from step 2 (or Make's SMS module if the shop prefers the review-link step outside the voice tool). The Google review link comes from the owner's own Business Profile — five minutes in the dashboard, free.

Step 4 — The owner replies to every review

4Drafted by AI, posted by a human

New reviews get a reply within 24 hours — the compiled BrightLocal 2025 figures put readers-of-replies at 97% of review readers, and reply behavior is one of the few ranking-adjacent signals a shop fully controls (compiled local-SEO reference, crawled October 2026). The automation drafts a reply from the review's own text — naming the service and the tech, never a template sentence twice — and the owner reviews and posts it with one tap. Copy-paste replies are a trust negative; the BrightLocal 2026 survey found half of consumers reject them. The unhappy-customer replies from branch B get the same treatment once the owner has actually fixed the problem — a resolved complaint with a thoughtful reply converts better than a silent five-star.

Built with: Make — new-review watch, draft generation from the review text, owner-approval queue. The owner posts; the AI never does.

Step 5 — The monthly velocity check

5Count reviews like leads

Once a month the owner opens a one-page dashboard: reviews added this month, average rating, and the same two numbers for the three closest competitors' Google profiles. The working rule from local-SEO practice is to match the top competitor's monthly review count and add one (compiled local-SEO reference, crawled October 2026) — that's the velocity target the sequence tunes against. Below ~20 total reviews the playbook's job is volume (47% of consumers won't consider a business under 20, per BrightLocal 2026); past that, the job is recency and rating defense. When a competitor runs a burst campaign, the steady system already in place is the answer — the retest data says bursts fade and steady wins.

Built with: a spreadsheet or the dispatch board's review report + 10 minutes a month. The automation feeds the count; the owner sets the target.

Common objections, answered honestly

Isn't a review link in a text enough? Why add an AI call?

For a small shop it is — and the page says so in step 3's honest read. The AI call earns its cost at volume: it splits happy from unhappy customers before the link goes out, so complaints reach the owner instead of Google. A text can't do the sentiment split; a human can't do it at 200 jobs a month. The call is the filter, not the ask.

We already run playbook #1 — isn't this the same system?

Same tools, different job. #1 is inbound: a missed call happens, the system calls back within 60 seconds. #6 is post-job: a finished job triggers a check-in call hours later. They share the voice-agent stack but they never share a trigger, a script, or a metric — #1 measures recovered calls, #6 measures review velocity. Most shops should run #1 first (missed calls are the bigger leak) and add #6 when the phone is handled.

What about Yelp?

Yelp's guidance discourages asking for reviews at all, and Yelp filters solicited-looking reviews aggressively — so this playbook's ask targets Google only, where 71% of review readers already look (BrightLocal 2026). Don't game Yelp; the Google profile is the asset that compounds.

Why not just ask the happy customers and skip the unhappy ones?

That is review gating, defined — and it's the exact practice Google's policy forbids and the FTC's rule penalizes. Beyond the legal exposure, it's a losing strategy: an unhappy customer you never hear from writes the one-star anyway, and now you never saw it coming. The recovery path is the whole point of step 2.

Will Google flag AI-drafted replies?

The AI drafts; the owner posts — that's the design, and it's the honest one. Google's business-profile API explicitly provides an owner-reply function, so owner-posted replies are the permitted act. What the policy forbids is fabricated customer content and manipulated reviews — this playbook writes neither. Keep the drafts individuated (half of consumers reject copy-paste replies, per BrightLocal 2026) and keep the human in the loop.
