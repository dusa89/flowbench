The honesty gates: a document machine, never a compliance authority

This playbook automates the paper chase around subcontractor insurance — it never replaces the people who decide what the paperwork must say. Five gates, printed before the verdict, non-negotiable. Gate 1: your broker drafts the requirements, not this page. Coverage types, limits, additional-insured wording, and endorsement forms (CG 20 10, CG 20 37) come from your insurance broker or counsel, per contract and per state — a playbook cannot write your risk profile. Gate 2: a certificate of insurance is not coverage. The ACORD form proves a policy existed on paper when it was issued; it does not grant coverage, guarantee the wording on endorsements, or survive a mid-policy cancellation. Verification means reading the certificate against the requirements, not filing it. Gate 3: collect from your own subs and partners only. This machine runs on the subcontractors you signed — it never buys certificate data from brokers or lists, and it never hunts documents for relationships that do not exist. Gate 4: payment holds must live in the contract. You can only withhold payment on non-compliant subs if your subcontract says so — a hold without a contract clause is a dispute waiting to happen. Gate 5: vendor numbers are vendor numbers. The error-rate and time-saving figures below come from companies that sell COI software — they are labeled as vendor claims, not benchmarks, and they set the ceiling, not the promise.

The verdict: every sub documented before day one, and stayed documented

The most expensive line on a general contractor's insurance audit is the subcontractor you paid but cannot prove was insured. An insurance auditor does not care that your sub "had insurance" — he cares whether you can produce the certificate for every day that sub worked under your name. A sub who cannot prove he carried his own valid coverage for every day on your job rolls into your payroll at your class rate — 12, 18, sometimes 25 percent of every dollar you paid him (Scott Fox insurance channel, Sep 2026). That is the machine this playbook builds: a document intake that starts the day the sub signs, an automated verification against your broker's requirements, an expiration radar that catches renewals and the quiet mid-policy cancellation, and a payment-hold gate that enforces it all.

Why this works as a build: a 2026 pricing guide for COI-tracking software notes that automation works here because every certificate follows the standardized ACORD 25 and ACORD 27 forms, which place coverage data in consistent fields that software can extract reliably (vertikalrms.com, 2026). Standardized documents are the ideal fuel for an automation — and the 23% of certificates that a COI vendor says contain errors or coverage gaps when reviewed manually are exactly what automated checking catches (myCOI, vendor claim, crawled Sep 2026).

Step 0 — the two rules

0Requirements before collection, collection before site day

Rule one: the broker writes the rulebook. Before you build anything, get the written insurance requirements per contract type — coverage types, limits, additional-insured wording, endorsement forms, and the states the sub works in. That document is the configuration this whole machine runs on; the machine never invents it. Rule two: no sub sets foot on site until the packet is complete and verified. A GC interviewed by a COI vendor put it plainly: "we won't let subcontractors on site until they have their insurance in place" and "we won't release payments until an insurance certificate is compliant" — because coverage is a moving target and a sub "may be compliant one week and not the next" (getjones.com, 2026).

Step 1 — the requirements pack

1Turn the broker's rulebook into a checklist the machine can run

Translate the broker's requirements into a machine-readable checklist: which coverage types (GL, workers' comp, auto, umbrella), the minimum limits, the additional-insured wording, the endorsement forms, the policy date window for the project, and which documents must arrive (certificate, endorsements, W-9). Store it as structured data — one checklist per project type, not per sub. Tooling: Make (the scenario engine that everything below runs on).

Step 2 — the intake lane

2A frictionless upload lane for subs and their brokers

The moment a sub signs, the machine sends the requirements pack and a single upload link — no accounts, no logins. The friction lesson from the COI-software market: tools that force subcontractors to create accounts measurably reduce compliance rates (getbcs.com comparison, crawled Oct 2026). The sub — or their broker, who is usually faster — uploads the certificate and endorsements straight into the lane. Day 1 auto-reminder, Day 3 nudge, Day 7 firm note, all from the same sequence; nobody on your team chases a PDF. Tooling: Make (intake flow, reminders, storage).

Step 3 — automated verification

3Read every certificate against the checklist — instantly

Each upload gets read against the Step-0 checklist: all required coverage types present, limits at or above minimums, your company named as additional insured, policy dates covering the project window, business name matching the contract. Because ACORD 25/27 forms put coverage data in consistent fields, this is a pattern-matching job, not a reading job (vertikalrms.com, 2026). Pass = green, start date issued. Fail = the sub gets a specific deficiency list ("GL per-occurrence limit shows $1M; contract requires $2M") instead of a vague "insurance issue" — the specificity is what kills the back-and-forth that the Jones interview describes as the core delay (getjones.com, 2026). Human override always available; the machine drafts, your admin approves exceptions. Tooling: Make (AI document-read modules).

Step 4 — the chase engine

4Voice calls for the subs who ghost the reminders

Email reminders die in busy seasons. The subs who go quiet after Day 7 get an AI voice call — it self-identifies in the first sentence, names the exact missing document, and offers to text the upload link live on the call. This is the family's voice-leg pattern applied to back-office paper, not customers: same consent-aware machinery, different job. The call is logged to the sub's record; three failed chases escalate to the PM with the sub's start date at risk. Tooling: Synthflow (AI voice calls) + ElevenLabs (voices).

Step 5 — the expiration radar

560/30/7-day radar plus the mid-policy cancellation catch

Day-one verification is worthless without surveillance — policies lapse, renewals slip, and as the Scott Fox case shows, a policy can die quietly in March while the sub keeps showing up. The radar runs three checks: (1) 60/30/7-day renewal reminders before each policy expires, routed to the sub and the broker; (2) a monthly re-verification sweep of active subs' coverage against the checklist — this is what catches the mid-policy cancellation; (3) a status board your PMs can see at a glance, per project, so no non-compliant sub ever dispatches. Tooling: Make (scheduled scenarios, reminders, status board).

Step 6 — the payment-hold gate and the audit packet

6Enforce compliance through the pay cycle, then hand the auditor a packet

The enforcement lever that makes the whole machine credible: a sub whose compliance status is red cannot be paid — the hold is written into the subcontract (Gate 4), and the machine checks compliance status before any pay application clears. "We won't release payments until an insurance certificate is compliant" (getjones.com, 2026) stops being a policy and starts being a system. Monthly, the machine compiles the compliance ledger per project; at audit time, you hand the auditor a complete certificate packet — every sub, every coverage day, every policy — instead of a spreadsheet and a prayer. Tooling: Make (pay-gate webhook + ledger reports).

Questions this page must answer

Is this legal or insurance advice?

No. This is an automation playbook — it builds the machine that runs your broker's requirements, it never writes them. Insurance requirements vary by state, contract, and carrier; your broker or counsel drafts the checklist this machine enforces.

Can AI actually read a certificate of insurance accurately?

It reads the data fields well — ACORD 25/27 forms place coverage data in consistent fields, which is why a 2026 pricing guide says automation works here (vertikalrms.com, 2026). But a certificate is not coverage: endorsement wording (CG 20 10, CG 20 37), additional-insured language, and exclusions still deserve a human eye on the exceptions. The machine drafts; your admin approves.

What about wrap-ups (OCIP/CCIP) where the owner provides coverage?

Enrolled subs are covered by the project policy — the machine handles them as an exception track: proof of enrollment instead of a certificate, with the same expiration radar for enrollment end dates. The checklist from Step 0 must define the wrap rules before you build.

What happens when a sub ghosts all the reminders?

Day 7 the AI voice call runs; three failed chases escalate to the project manager with the sub's scheduled start date attached. The machine's honest limit: it can make ignoring impossible, not compliance automatic. The payment-hold gate is the enforcement that finishes the job.

Does this help at insurance-audit time?

That is the entire point. The auditor's question is always "produce the certificate for every day this sub worked." Step 6's audit packet — every sub, every coverage day, every policy — is the machine's output formatted for exactly that question, including the mid-policy lapses the radar caught.
