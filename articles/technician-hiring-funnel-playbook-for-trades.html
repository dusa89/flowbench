The hiring-law gates: AI screens, humans hire — five gates, non-negotiable

Hiring is the most regulated lane in this playbook library, and the hard box here is load-bearing, not decorative. Five gates, printed before the verdict. Gate 1: the AI never makes a hiring decision. It places the screening call, asks the structured questions, and scores factual job-related answers. Every decision — who interviews, who gets a trial day, who gets an offer — is made by a human. This is the design that keeps you in the lowest-risk posture: New York City's Local Law 144 (in effect since July 5, 2023) treats automated employment decision tools as a regulated category — employers using them must disclose the AI use to candidates, commission an annual independent bias audit, publish a summary, and offer an alternative selection process on request, with fines of $500 for a first violation and $1,500 for subsequent ones (Bloomberg Law; HCAmag). Tools that only assist while a human decides are a different posture than tools that decide — so keep the human in the decision seat, on purpose, in writing. Gate 2: structured, job-related questions only. License and certifications, years in the trade, EPA 608 for HVAC, a valid driver's license, reliable transportation, availability for the on-call rotation, pay expectations. Nothing that touches a protected class, nothing about health or disability, nothing the EEOC would ask about. The EEOC's rule of thumb: if a selection step advances a protected group at less than 80% of the rate of the most-selected group, that gap may indicate bias (the four-fifths rule — Bloomberg Law). Gate 3: the automated call announces itself in the first sentence and gets consent to continue; anyone who declines gets a human callback (the alternative path — good practice everywhere, a legal requirement under NYC's law). Some states layer their own consent and data-deletion rules on AI in hiring — check your state's rules before you record or store anything. Gate 4: keep the receipts. Every score, every knockout reason, every decision, logged in the shortlist sheet. If a selection-rate gap ever appears in your own funnel, you have the data to see it — and the audit trail is what separates a structured process from a biased one. Gate 5: the vendor-adjacent numbers are self-reported. SHRM's figures below come from teams that already adopted AI recruiting (ceiling-labeled); Bullhorn's 92% comes from candidates who went through staffing firms, not trade shops (labeled). Read the gates before you quote the numbers.

The verdict: in a 3-to-1 market, speed is the hiring strategy

Your shop's real competitor for every technician is not the shop across town — it is the data center down the road paying more, the 56-day average fill time, and the five retirees leaving for every two entrants arriving. In that market, the shop that answers the applicant first wins the hire more often than the shop with the better offer letter. This playbook builds an always-on funnel: one evergreen posting per role, a five-minute AI screening call that reaches every applicant the same day, a scored shortlist the owner reviews in ten minutes each morning, a paid trial day that replaces the recruiter's guarantee, and a 30-60-90 retention loop so the hire you made in October is still on the truck in February. The machine's whole job is Step 2's speed — an applicant who waits 48 hours for a call has taken another offer, and the funnel makes sure nobody ever waits.

Why this works as a build: the AGC/NCCER 2026 Workforce Survey (1,830 firms, July–August 2026) found 87% of contractors reporting hourly craft openings and 88% saying those positions are as hard or harder to fill than a year ago — while Bullhorn's September 2026 survey of nearly 2,300 candidates found 92% rated an AI voice interview as good as or better than a human recruiter interview. The applicants are not afraid of the AI call; they are afraid of never hearing back. This funnel is the answer to the exact frustration candidates name: a response to every application, fast.

Step 0 — the two rules

0The two rules

Rule 1: AI screens, humans hire. The machine's jurisdiction ends at the scored shortlist. It places the call, asks the eight questions, records the answers, and applies the rubric the owner wrote. The owner decides who gets a trial day and who gets an offer — Gate 1 is the architecture, not a slogan. Write that boundary into the runbook so it survives staff turnover.

Rule 2: speed is the strategy. An applicant who applies to five shops on Tuesday night interviews with the two that called back by Wednesday. The funnel's whole job is a same-day first touch — every hour of silence is an hour the candidate is someone else's interview. Measure time-to-first-contact on every applicant; the funnel is broken if the median exceeds 24 hours.

Tools: the runbook (one page, laminated in the office) + the time-to-first-contact metric in the shortlist sheet (Step 3).

Step 1 — the always-on intake

1The always-on intake

Keep one evergreen posting per role live at all times — service tech, installer, apprentice — on Indeed and surfaced to Google for Jobs, even when you are not actively hiring. A funnel that only opens during a crisis starts cold every time; a funnel that is always warm has a bench when a truck needs a second pair of hands. Layer the intake sources: a QR code on every truck and jobsite sign ("We're hiring techs — 60 seconds to apply") that opens the same intake form; "we're hiring" posts on the Google Business Profile; one trade-school contact per trade fed a standing invitation to send graduates; and an employee referral bonus (the cheapest source of quality hires you will ever pay). One intake form for all sources, job-related fields only: name, phone, trade, years of experience, license/certifications held, EPA 608 (HVAC), valid driver's license, reliable transportation, availability for the on-call rotation, pay expectation, and consent to be contacted. Every application lands in one sheet with a timestamp — the clock starts there.

Tools: Indeed organic posting (free tier) + Google for Jobs surface; a QR generator (free); the intake form (any form tool); a Make scenario (verified affiliate leg, make.com/en/affiliate, checked Oct 1–2, 2026): new application → timestamp → trigger Step 2.

Step 2 — the 5-minute AI screening call (the funnel's whole job)

2The 5-minute AI screening call

Within five minutes of a daytime application (next morning for overnight applications), the applicant gets a call — from the AI, announced as automated in the first sentence, with consent to continue (Gate 3). The script is eight structured questions, identical for every applicant, written by the owner: (1) trade and years of experience; (2) license/certifications held; (3) EPA 608 status, for HVAC; (4) valid driver's license; (5) reliable transportation to the shop; (6) availability for the on-call rotation; (7) pay expectation; (8) earliest start date. The owner sets the knockouts in advance — for example, no required state license, or no transportation — and everything else scores 1 to 5 against the rubric. The AI never rejects anyone; it scores and routes. Anyone who declines the automated call gets a human callback the same day (the alternative path). The reason this works on applicants: Bullhorn's 2026 survey found candidates' top-valued AI benefits were a response to every application and a fast process — the screening call is the opposite of the black-hole application portals trades are infamous for.

Tools: Synthflow (verified affiliate leg, official partner terms, checked Oct 1–2, 2026) or ElevenLabs (verified affiliate leg, elevenlabs.io/affiliates-terms, checked Oct 1–2, 2026) for the voice call; Make (verified) for the 5-minute trigger → call → score-to-sheet routing. The rubric lives in the shortlist sheet, owned by the human.

Step 3 — the scored shortlist (the owner's 10-minute morning)

3The scored shortlist

Every morning the owner spends ten minutes on the shortlist sheet: applicant, rubric score, knockout flags, pay expectation, and a link to the call transcript for anyone on the bubble. The owner sets the invite-to-trial-day threshold — say, 30 out of 40 — and invites everyone above it. Below-threshold applicants get the honest decline message the same day (the funnel answers everyone — the candidates who remember you next time are the ones you treated well this time). Every score, knockout reason, and decision is logged (Gate 4 — the audit trail). The red-flag review: before any trial day is offered, the owner skims the transcript of bubble candidates for what the rubric cannot see — attitude toward customers, safety habits described in their own words, why they left the last shop.

Tools: the shortlist sheet (one tab: scores; one tab: decisions log); a Make scenario (verified) that sends the morning digest — new applicants, scores, transcript links — to the owner's phone at 7am.

Step 4 — the paid trial day (your guarantee, better than the recruiter's)

4The paid trial day

Shortlisted candidates get a four-hour paid ride-along and shadow shift with a senior tech, plus one hands-on bench task matched to the trade — braze a joint, wire a contactor, diagnose a staged fault on a training unit. The senior tech fills out the evaluation card: safety habits, tool familiarity, communication with the (consenting) customer, pace. Pay for the day — never free labor; the trial day is a working interview, and the shop that pays for four hours learns more about a hire than the shop that reads forty resumes. This is the step the recruiter's guarantee cannot match: a contingency recruiter sells you a 60-to-90-day replacement guarantee (legalclarity.org, 2026 fee-model guide) — the trial day is your guarantee, observed with your own eyes, for the cost of four hours of tech time instead of 15 to 25% of a first-year salary. Candidates who pass the trial day get the offer within 48 hours — Rule 2 does not end at the application.

Tools: the one-page evaluation card (safety / tools / customer communication / pace, 1–5 each, plus the senior tech's one-line verdict); the bench task kit per trade, kept staged in the shop.

Step 5 — the 30-60-90 retention loop (hiring solves October; retention solves the year)

5The 30-60-90 retention loop

The funnel's metric is not applications — it is 90-day retention of trial-day hires. Day 7, 30, 60, and 90, the new hire gets a check-in call (AI-assisted, human-reviewed): what is working, what is missing, what would make you stay. Every new hire is assigned a mentor on day one — one senior tech, named, accountable. The model to copy: Ace Electric in Valdosta, Georgia, runs a weeklong "Ace University" orientation for new hires (via fieldnews.net, September 2026) — structured onboarding in a trade where most shops hand a new tech a van key and a prayer. The loop's prevention side: log every early departure's reason in the sheet next to the hiring data, and review quarterly — if trial-day hires keep leaving at day 45 for pay, the funnel's pay-expectation field in Step 1 was telling you the market price all along.

Tools: the check-in call script (4 calls, 5 questions each); a Make scenario (verified) that schedules the Day 7/30/60/90 calls from the hire date; the mentor assignment board.

Family fit: why this is not a re-skin

All 20 prior playbooks are customer-side or back-office jobs — missed calls, quotes, reviews, no-shows, invoices, renewals, referrals, storms, compliance, content. None of them hire anyone; every one of them quietly assumes the truck is staffed. The closest cousin is playbook #5 (after-hours dispatch triage) — same AI voice channel, opposite side of the business: #5 answers customers who call you, this one reaches applicants who found you. ("#1 catches the calls you missed; this catches the hires you keep missing.") Different trigger (a job application vs a customer event), different goal (staffing trucks vs revenue operations), different automation map (intake → screen → shortlist → trial day → retention loop), different metric (90-day retention vs dollars recovered). This is also the family's first workforce playbook — and its deepest compound: the technicians this funnel hires are the technicians who film playbook #21's jobsite videos, run playbook #11's on-site upsells, answer playbook #1's recovered calls, and enroll playbook #8's maintenance plans. Questions this page must answer

Won't applicants just hang up on an AI call?

The best available evidence says no: 92% of candidates interviewed by an AI voice agent rated the experience as good as or better than a human recruiter interview, and candidates named "a response to every application" as one of AI's top benefits (Bullhorn GRID 2026, ~2,300 candidates, September 23, 2026). The call announces itself, asks eight job questions, and takes five minutes — then anyone who prefers a human gets a human callback the same day. The thing applicants actually hate is the black hole: applying and never hearing back.

Is this legal in my state?

Read the hard box: the design — AI screens, humans decide; structured job-related questions; consent and an alternative path; every decision logged — is the lowest-risk posture under the current rules, including NYC's Local Law 144 (disclosure, annual independent bias audit, published summary, $500/$1,500 fines) and the EEOC's four-fifths rule of thumb. But hiring law is state-specific and moving, and this page is not legal advice. Talk to employment counsel before you automate any hiring decision, and re-check the rules annually.

We only hire one or two techs a year. Is this overkill?

That is exactly who it is for. The funnel idles at zero marginal cost — the evergreen posting stays live, the intake stays warm, the screening call fires only when someone applies. A shop that hires twice a year cannot afford a recruiter's 20% fee twice a year, and it cannot afford to lose either hire to a 48-hour silence. The pipeline is warm when the truck needs a second pair of hands.

What does the AI actually ask? Give me the script.

Eight questions, identical for every applicant: trade and years of experience; license and certifications held; EPA 608 status (HVAC); valid driver's license; reliable transportation to the shop; availability for the on-call rotation; pay expectation; earliest start date. The owner sets the knockouts in advance and the 1-to-5 rubric. Job-related, structured, the same for everyone — that is the whole point.

How do I keep the tech from leaving in 90 days?

Step 5 is half the playbook: Day 7/30/60/90 check-in calls, a named mentor on day one, and a structured first week modeled on Ace Electric's "Ace University" orientation (fieldnews.net, September 2026). And the funnel feeds itself: log every early departure's reason next to the hiring data — the pay expectations your applicants stated in Step 1 are the market telling you its price. The funnel's metric is 90-day retention of trial-day hires, not raw applications.
