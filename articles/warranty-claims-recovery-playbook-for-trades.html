The warranty-truth gates: five gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before the first claim is filed. Gate 1: never promise coverage to the customer. The manufacturer decides what is covered, not the shop — quoting 10 years on an unregistered unit hands the tech the bill when the claim is denied. Gate 2: the part is free; the repair is not. Manufacturer warranties cover the physical part — labor, freight, refrigerant, diagnostic fees, and consumables are excluded (Daikin's own warranty document: "Daikin will not pay for labor, freight, or any other cost"). Gate 3: register inside the window or lose half the term. 60 to 90 days after installation, depending on the brand — miss it and 10 years of parts coverage becomes 5. Gate 4: hold the failed part until the claim closes. Manufacturers keep a 30-day inspection right; a shop that scraps the failed compressor on day 2 has no evidence when the claim is disputed. Gate 5: denied claims are the shop's cost. Price the chase — never bet margin on an approval you don't have yet.

The verdict: file every eligible claim like it's a receivable

Because it is. The system: register every install inside the brand's 60/90-day window, run a warranty check at triage before dispatch, build the claim kit (serial evidence, photos, install invoice, service history) at diagnosis, chase every open claim on an automated 7-day cycle, and keep a monthly recovered-dollars ledger. The directional evidence (third-party, dated, below) says the leak is structural — registration skipped, coverage unchecked, claims unfiled — not a one-shop problem. The verdict that counts is your own 90-day before/after on the 4-number ledger in Step 5.

1Register every install inside the window — the 60/90-day ritual

Gate 3's rule made into a ritual: every install is registered within the brand's window, no exceptions, no "the customer will do it." The install invoice carries the serial number, the install date, and a registration-receipt checkbox the office signs off before the job file closes. The registration receipt is filed on the asset record (riooapp's second leak: terms living in an email or a binder instead of on the equipment). The ~1/3 registration stat is the wedge — a shop that registers 100% of its own installs is already beating two-thirds of the trade. Minimum viable start: a spreadsheet with install date, serial, brand, registration deadline, and receipt saved. This step is $0 and it is where half the money on this page lives.

2Run the warranty check at triage — before dispatch, not after

The third leak is a workflow problem: the work order goes out and nobody checks coverage. The fix is one line on the triage script: unit age vs term, on the ticket, before the truck rolls. The dispatcher (or the AI triage agent — playbook #1's missed-call voice flow is the same slot) pulls the install date and serial from the customer record, checks the brand registry, and marks the ticket: in-warranty, out-of-warranty, or unknown. The tech arrives knowing whether the failed part is a claim file or a retail sale — never discovering it on the driveway. "Unknown" is an honest answer and triggers the registry check from the truck, not a guess.

Cross-link: Playbook #1 (missed-call recovery) owns the triage moment — the warranty check joins the same call.

3Build the claim kit at diagnosis

When the part fails inside the warranty window, the tech builds the kit before leaving the site: (1) serial-number photo of the failed part and the unit; (2) failure photo — the crack, the burn, the seized bearing; (3) the install invoice proving the install date; (4) the service history — maintenance records are the denial shield, because unmaintained equipment is the easiest claim to deny. Then Gate 4: the failed part is bagged, tagged, and held 30 days — the manufacturer keeps an inspection right, and a shop that scraps the evidence on day 2 has nothing to appeal with. File the claim within the distributor's window; run the per-part-class math first (the $15/$50/$100 processing-fee problem): filing a $40 contactor claim that costs $15 in fees plus 23 minutes of admin is a loss — batch the cheap parts monthly.

4Chase the claim status — and run the denied-claim drill

Open claims get an automated 7-day status check: a Make scenario off the CRM's claim-status field pings the distributor portal or emails the distributor rep; anything past 21 days open escalates to a Synthflow AI voice call to the distributor (affiliate placeholders) — "claim number X, filed Y, still open — what's the hold?" The fieldproxy ceiling (45 days to 12) is the direction, not the promise. Then the drill nobody writes down: the denied-claim drill. A denied claim gets one appeal with the full kit — never a shrug. If the appeal fails, the shop costs the part honestly (Gate 5): the denied part is the shop's cost, logged against the job, never passed to the customer as a surprise line. The drill's real product is the rejection-reason log — three denials for the same missing document is a training problem, not a manufacturer problem.

Build path: Make 35%/12mo + Synthflow 30%/15mo + ElevenLabs 22%/12mo (program terms verified Oct 1-2, 2026) — affiliate placeholders, confirm before buying.

5Keep the monthly recovered-dollars ledger

Four numbers, reviewed monthly: (1) registration completion rate on new installs (target: 100% inside the window — the ritual from Step 1); (2) claim approval rate — approved claims vs filed; (3) days to reimbursement — file date to credit date; (4) dollars recovered — the headline, parts dollars credited back. Plus one honest fifth line: warranty labor cost — the tech hours the manufacturer will never reimburse (Gate 2). The ledger's job is to prove the playbook pays: recovered dollars minus admin cost, per month, trending. If approval rate craters, the claim kit (Step 3) is broken — usually the service-history line. If days-to-reimbursement stretches, the chase (Step 4) isn't firing.

Honest weak points

(1) The fieldproxy numbers are vendor blueprints. 34% more reimbursable revenue, 73% fewer rejections, 45 days to 12 — a workflow-automation vendor's own case math. The ceiling, not the typical shop. (2) The 1/3 registration stat is one wholesaler's account. Directionally devastating, not a surveyed rate — the shop's own registration audit (Step 1's first month) is the real number. (3) Labor is never recovered. This playbook recovers parts dollars; the manufacturer's check never covers the tech's two hours. Shops that price warranty work at zero labor are donating — the fifth ledger line exists for exactly this. (4) Per-part-class math is mandatory. A $15 processing fee on a cheap part can make filing a net loss — batch the small parts, file the big ones. (5) Term splits vary by brand and series. Goodman/Amana 5/10, Daikin 5/12, Trane and Carrier families with their own registered/unregistered schedules — never quote a term from memory; the brand's registry wins.

Questions shops actually ask

How is this different from the overdue-invoice recovery playbook?

#9 (overdue-invoice recovery) collects from customers — its object is the unpaid invoice, its trigger is terms crossed, its metric is days-sales-outstanding. This playbook collects from manufacturers — its object is the claim file, its trigger is a failed part inside warranty, its metric is recovered dollars plus approval rate. Different payer, different paperwork, different failure mode; they compound (a shop that collects its invoices and its claims has no money left on the table).

How is this different from the end-of-life equipment ledger?

#18 (end-of-life ledger) plans replacements — its object is the aging unit, its trigger is lifespan-minus-3, its metric is planned installs on the customer's schedule. This playbook files claims on failed parts — reactive, not proactive. #18's ledger rows carry the warranty window; this playbook spends it.

How is this different from the parts-ready van stock playbook?

#23 (van stock) stocks parts forward — its object is the binned van, its trigger is the Friday restock flag, its metric is first-time fix rate. This playbook files paperwork backward — its object is the failed part's claim file. One keeps the van ready; the other gets the manufacturer to pay for what the van consumed.

What about the labor on warranty jobs?

Honestly: the manufacturer never pays it. Gate 2 exists because shops learn this on a $900 labor invoice against a "covered" repair. The playbook tracks warranty labor as the fifth ledger line — the true cost of warranty work — so the shop can decide whether to sell extended labor plans at install (a separate decision, not this playbook's job) with real numbers instead of vibes.

Does this work outside HVAC?

Yes — parts warranties exist across plumbing, electrical, appliance, garage door, and elevator service (the fieldproxy blueprints cover three of those trades). HVAC is the worked example because the 10/5-year registration split is the clearest demand signal, but the 5 steps are trade-agnostic: register, check at triage, kit at diagnosis, chase the status, ledger the dollars.
