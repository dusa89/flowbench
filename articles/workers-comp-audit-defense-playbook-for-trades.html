The audit-truth gates: five gates, non-negotiable

Read the gates before the audit letter arrives. Gate 1: the audit is a true-up, not a punishment. Your premium started as an estimate of payroll by class code; the audit matches it to what actually happened. Bills go down too — skipping the audit is how you pay the maximum. Gate 2: a certificate on file is not proof of coverage. Policies cancel mid-year and the carrier has no duty to tell you; a sub who carried coverage in January and lost it in March is uninsured labor at audit time. Gate 3: mixed duties, one class code, is the most expensive way to classify. If your records don't split payroll by exposure, the auditor classifies it under the most expensive applicable code — the tech who does office work on Fridays becomes a full-time roofer on paper. Gate 4: never ignore the audit letter. Non-response doesn't cancel the bill; it lets the carrier write one from its own estimates. Gate 5: this is a records playbook, not an insurance license. The ex-mod fight, the class-code fight, and coverage decisions belong to your licensed agent — the shop's job is to hand the auditor a file so clean there is nothing to fight about.

The verdict: hand the auditor a file so clean the bill writes itself small

The system: map every role to the right class code before the auditor does, build a quarterly payroll-snapshot packet the whole year long, chase sub certificates 30 days before the audit letter lands, read the audit worksheet line by line with the three appeal paths ready, and true-up estimated payroll mid-year instead of discovering the gap at audit. The directional evidence (third-party, dated, below) says the surprise bill is a records problem — uninsured sub payroll absorbed at your class rate, misclassified staff, overtime never flagged, a worksheet nobody read. The verdict that counts is your own first clean audit: the bill, itemized, against the estimate.

1Build the class-code map — before the auditor builds it for you

Gate 3's ritual: every person in the company has a class code on paper, matched to what they actually do, reviewed at every hire and every role change. The map is a one-page table: name, title, actual duties, class code, who set it, when. The three fights this wins: the office manager who does dispatch work vs pure 8810 clerical; the tech who sells (outside-sales 8742 is a different animal than service 5183); the officer who does zero field work but sits in a field class. Minimum viable start: print the ICW checklist line — "if your records do not break down payroll by different workplace exposures, the auditor will classify it under the most expensive classification applicable" — and tape it to the map. This step is $0 and it is where the misclassification money lives. Cross-check the map against the quarterly 941s every 90 days: total payroll on the map must tie to payroll on the 941s, or the auditor will find the difference first.

Tooling: a spreadsheet now; Make or n8n later — new hire in the HR row triggers a "class-code assigned" row on the map before day one, not after the audit letter.

2Run the quarterly payroll-snapshot ritual — the packet builds itself

The audit doesn't have to be an archaeology dig. Four times a year, 30 minutes: drop the quarter's payroll summary, the 941s, overtime flagged separately, and every sub/temp payment with its COI attached, into a folder named YYYY-Qn-audit-packet. Overtime gets its own line because the auditor is allowed to discount premium overtime to straight time — but only if your records make it readable (the auditor "does not have the time to perform the premium portion," per the carrier checklist). Sub payments carry three attachments: the 1099 total, the COI current for the whole quarter, and the verification screenshot from the state portal. The folder is the audit packet; the audit meeting is a handoff, not a search party. Automate the boring part: the payroll export, the reminder, the folder creation, and the missing-item nag all run on a schedule. The human's only job is the 30-minute eyeball.

Tooling: Make/n8n — quarterly scheduled pull of the payroll export + reminder sequence until the packet folder is complete; Synthflow or ElevenLabs voice for the nag nobody wants to make.

3Chase the sub certificates 30 days before the audit letter — not after

The $5,000 story is a timing problem: the sub's policy died in March and the GC's system never looked. Thirty days before the policy renews, run the cancellation sweep: every sub COI re-verified on the state portal, email tracking re-registered for every tracked policy, expired and cancelled certificates chased by voice, not by email they'll ignore. The chase script has three moves: the friendly 30-day notice, the mid-policy cancellation catch (the portal email alert firing because you registered it — Georgia's OCI ritual, done for every state), and the payment-hold gate — no current verified COI, no check cut. This step is where playbook #20's COI machine plugs in: this playbook is the audit; #20 is the compliance engine that feeds it. If you don't have #20 yet, the minimum viable version is the spreadsheet version: sub name, COI effective dates, verification date, tracker registered (yes/no).

Tooling: Playbook #20 (subcontractor COI compliance) is the full machine — expiration radar, automated verification, the voice-agent chase. This playbook runs its pre-audit sweep on top of it.

4Run the worksheet-review drill when the bill lands — read before you pay

The audit worksheet is not a verdict; it's a draft. Line-by-line against your packet: class codes against your Step-1 map, payroll totals against your 941s, sub payroll against your COI file, overtime discounted, and the ex-mod math spot-checked. The three appeal paths, in order: (1) worksheet correction — "here's the record that says this line is wrong," fastest and most common; (2) ex-mod review — 1 in 3 carry mistakes and a wrong mod follows you to every bid, so the review pays for itself even when the premium doesn't move; (3) formal carrier appeal — slow, documented, worth it only when the records are airtight. Never argue a line you can't prove from the packet; the auditor can't either, and that symmetry is the whole game. File the worksheet, your corrections, and the outcome in the same folder as the packet — next year's audit starts from this year's ending.

Tooling: n8n — the audit letter's arrival (email parser) triggers the worksheet-review checklist, assigns owners, and opens the appeal log.

5True-up mid-year — stop discovering the gap at audit

The $8,000 surprise bill is what happens when the estimate from January meets the reality of December with no conversation in between. Twice a year, 15 minutes with your agent: headcount up or down, payroll vs estimate, sub spend vs estimate, any new operations or states. Growing sales is good news — it just shouldn't be news to your carrier in December. The mid-year true-up adjusts the estimated premium on the policy instead of deferring the whole difference to the audit, which means the audit becomes a reconciliation of small numbers instead of a reveal of big ones. Put the true-up on the calendar as a recurring invite with the agent; the automation sends the payroll-vs-estimate summary to both parties the morning of the call.

Tooling: Make — the quarterly packet's totals auto-compared against the policy estimate; a variance over your threshold books the agent call and sends the summary.

Build vs buy

Build (the $0 start): a spreadsheet class-code map, four quarterly Drive folders, the state-portal verification ritual, and a calendar invite with your agent twice a year. That alone wins the three biggest fights on this page — misclassification, missing sub coverage, and the ignored audit letter. Buy the automation (when the packet takes more than 30 minutes a quarter): Make or n8n for the scheduled packet build and variance alerts (~their standard tiers), Synthflow or ElevenLabs for the voice chase on expired sub COIs (their standard per-minute voice pricing — confirm current rates on their sites). Buy the expertise (always): the ex-mod review and the class-code fight are licensed-agent territory — Willis's 1-in-5 and 1-in-3 numbers are a sales pitch for exactly this service, and sometimes the sales pitch is right. What you never buy: anyone who promises the bill will go down. The promise is a clean file, not a low number.

Honest weak points

First: refunds are the exception, not the rule. The 10% figure is 10% of clients who paid for an audit review — most audits confirm the bill. Second: California is its own planet — WCIRB rules, experience rating quirks, and rate dynamics don't transfer to NCCI states, and this page's California-flavored anecdotes shouldn't be read as national law. Third: the class-code fight needs a licensed agent, not a spreadsheet — the map is the shop's evidence, the agent is the argument. Fourth: ex-mod errors can take a full policy cycle to fix, so the review you run this year pays off next year. Fifth: sole-proprietor and owner-exclusion rules vary hard by state — officers on the map need state-specific advice, not this page. Sixth: this playbook assumes you have subs or employees; a true one-person shop with zero payroll exposure has no audit fight to win (Texas non-subscribers aside, which is a different page).

Questions shops actually ask

How is this different from the subcontractor COI compliance playbook?

Playbook #20's object is the compliance file — every sub documented before day one, kept documented all policy long, for general contractors. This page's object is the audit file — your own policy's estimated-vs-actual true-up: class codes, payroll records, overtime, the worksheet, the ex-mod. #20's Step 6 literally hands the auditor a packet; this playbook is what the auditor does with the packet, and the machinery around it. Different trigger (audit letter vs sub onboarding), different buyer (any contractor vs GCs), same enemy (the mid-year cancellation nobody tracked). They cross-link because #20 feeds this one.

How is this different from the overdue-invoice recovery playbook?

Playbook #9 collects money from customers — its trigger is crossed payment terms, its metric is days-sales-outstanding. This playbook defends money against the carrier — its trigger is the audit letter, its metric is the true-up bill (and the occasional refund). One chases receivables; the other audits payables. The ritual shape rhymes (chase engine, ledger, honesty gates) because both are money-recovery workflows, but the payer, the paperwork, and the opponent are all different.

I have employees but no subs. Does this still apply?

Yes — Steps 1, 2, 4, and 5 are employee-side. The class-code map and the payroll snapshot are pure W-2 territory, and misclassification hits employee-only shops exactly the same way (the office manager in a field class). You skip Step 3. The sub-COI fight is the loudest story on this page, but the quiet money is usually your own payroll in the wrong code.

Does this work in non-NCCI states, or in Texas?

The ritual transfers; the codes don't. Non-NCCI states (California, New York, New Jersey, and others) run their own bureaus with their own class codes and rules — the map, the packet, and the worksheet drill still apply, but every code number on this page must be re-mapped by your agent to your bureau. Texas non-subscription is a different animal entirely: no comp policy means no comp audit, but the liability trade-off needs an attorney, not a playbook.

The bill already landed. Do I pay it or fight it?

Read it first — that is Step 4's entire point. Most worksheets have at least one line worth a records-backed question, and corrections are the fastest money on this page. What you don't do: ignore it (the carrier writes its own version), or fight it without records (the auditor wins every argument you can't document). If the worksheet survives a clean read, pay it, file it, and build the packet so next year's doesn't.
